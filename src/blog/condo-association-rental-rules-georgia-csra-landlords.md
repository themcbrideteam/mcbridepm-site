## Georgia Has Two Separate Governing Frameworks for Residential Associations

Most homeowners and investors treat "HOA" as a single category. Georgia law does not. The state has two separate statutes that govern community associations, and they treat rental restrictions in meaningfully different ways.

**The Georgia Property Owners' Association Act (POAA)** — O.C.G.A. § 44-3-220 through § 44-3-235 — applies to planned subdivision communities that have voluntarily submitted to the Act. Evans, Grovetown, and many Columbia County neighborhoods with traditional subdivision HOAs are governed here. The POAA includes a specific leasing statute — O.C.G.A. § 44-3-226 — that limits how associations can restrict rentals and, critically, contains grandfathering protections for existing owners when an association adopts new restrictions. Our [HOA rental restrictions guide for CSRA landlords](/blog/georgia-hoa-rental-restrictions-csra-landlords/) covers the POAA framework in detail.

**The Georgia Condominium Act** — O.C.G.A. § 44-3-70 through [§ 44-3-115](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-44/chapter-3/article-3/) — governs condominium developments. This statute is separate from the POAA. It has its own compliance requirements, its own amendment process, and — crucially — it does not contain an equivalent of the POAA's § 44-3-226 grandfathering provision. A condo association that amends its declaration to restrict rentals can apply that restriction to every owner from the date of adoption, including those who voted against it.

Both statutes also override O.C.G.A. § 44-5-60, Georgia's general anti-restraint-on-alienation law that would otherwise prohibit restrictions on selling or leasing property. The exemption means both associations and condo communities can enforce rental restrictions that would be invalid outside a community-association context.

For rental investors, the takeaway is this: the same due-diligence process you'd apply to a subdivision HOA is insufficient for a condo. You need to read the condo-specific statute and the community's specific governing documents — not just ask whether there's an HOA.

## How Rental Restrictions Work Under the Condominium Act

Under the Georgia Condominium Act, a condo association's authority to restrict rentals comes from the community's **declaration** — the recorded legal document that created the condominium. The bylaws and rules also apply, but the declaration is the primary document.

A declaration can restrict rentals in several ways, and the specific language in your community's recorded documents is what governs. Common restriction types include:

**1. Outright prohibition.** Some declarations prohibit unit rentals entirely. This is common in communities originally developed as owner-occupied housing and later amended. An outright prohibition means you own a unit you legally cannot rent.

**2. Rental caps.** A cap limits the total percentage of units that can be rented at any time — frequently set at 15% to 35%. The reason is financial: FHA-insured loans and Fannie Mae-backed mortgages both have eligibility rules that limit lending in condo buildings where investor-rented units exceed a certain threshold (currently 50% for FHA in most cases, with stricter rules for some programs). An association at or near its FHA eligibility threshold has a financial incentive to cap rentals to protect the pool of eligible buyers — which also protects all owners' property values. If your unit is fine but the community is at cap, you cannot rent until another owner exits the rental pool.

**3. Board approval requirements.** The declaration or bylaws may require board approval before you rent. Criteria for approval are sometimes defined; sometimes they are not. Where criteria are undefined, the association exercises discretionary judgment — which can create inconsistency. This is one of the more commonly litigated provisions in Georgia condo law.

**4. Minimum lease term.** Many declarations require leases of at least six months, 12 months, or sometimes longer. Short-term and vacation rentals may be categorically prohibited. This affects your flexibility on lease structure and your ability to rent to month-to-month tenants.

**5. Tenant registration requirements.** Some associations require you to register tenants with the management office, provide copies of leases, or pay a tenant-registration or move-in fee. While these don't prohibit renting, they create administrative requirements and sometimes fees that affect your net operating income.

## The Grandfathering Problem: What the Condo Act Doesn't Give You

This is the most important distinction for CSRA rental investors who own or are buying condo units.

Under O.C.G.A. § 44-3-226 of the **POAA**, if a subdivision HOA community amends its declaration to add rental restrictions, existing owners who had no restriction when they purchased receive a grandfathering period during which the new restriction does not apply to them. The mechanics and duration of this protection depend on the specific statute language and the amendment terms, but the key point is that existing owners have a [statutory claim to protection](https://www.luederlaw.com/restricting-leasing-through-amendment/) from retroactive rental restrictions.

**The Georgia Condominium Act has no equivalent provision.** If a condo association adopts a valid amendment restricting rentals — with the required owner vote specified in the declaration — that amendment applies to all owners from its effective date. There is no Georgia statute that says a condo owner who bought before the restriction was adopted gets a grandfathering period.

This is not a hypothetical risk. In CSRA communities with rising owner-occupant percentages and concerns about investor concentration, rental restriction amendments can and do pass. If the community's declaration allows amendment by a two-thirds owner vote and two-thirds of owners prefer less investor activity, a restriction vote can succeed — and your rental income disappears.

The practical consequence: unlike a subdivision purchase, buying a condo unit as a rental does not give you a reasonable expectation that the rental right you have today will survive a community vote next year. Your return-on-investment calculation needs to include this risk.

This is also why vetting the community governance — not just the unit's numbers — is essential before you buy. A community where 70% of units are already owner-occupied and the board has discussed rental caps is a different risk profile from a community where 40% are investor-owned and the original declaration had permissive rental language.

## What SB 406 Does (and Doesn't Do) for Condo Landlords

The Georgia Property Owners' Bill of Rights Act, [SB 406](https://www.huntermaclean.com/2026/05/georgia-community-associations-face-major-regulatory-changes-under-sb-406/), was signed by Governor Kemp on May 12, 2026, as Act 715. Most provisions of the Act take effect January 1, 2027. SB 406 represents the most significant reform to Georgia community association law in years, and it covers both HOA communities and condominium associations.

Here is what SB 406 changes and what it leaves unchanged for CSRA condo rental investors:

| Provision | What Changes | Effective Date |
|-----------|-------------|---------------|
| Association registration | All associations must register annually with the Georgia Secretary of State. Failure to register prohibits collecting fines, fees, or accelerated assessments. | January 1, 2027 |
| Foreclosure threshold | Associations can only foreclose for liens after the amount reaches the lesser of $4,000 or 12 months of assessments, and not less than $2,000. | January 1, 2027 |
| Owner records access | Owners have a codified right to access association financial records and insurance information. | January 1, 2027 |
| Meetings and due process | Formal notice requirements and due-process expectations for hearings on violations and fines are codified. | January 1, 2027 |
| Attorney's fees | Prerequisites for charging attorney's fees and judicial review of reasonableness. | July 1, 2026 |
| Rental restrictions / grandfathering | **Not addressed.** SB 406 does not add a grandfathering provision to the Condominium Act equivalent to POAA § 44-3-226. | N/A |

The registration requirement is practically important for landlords: an association that hasn't registered with the Secretary of State as of January 1, 2027 is legally prohibited from collecting fines and fees. If you receive a violation notice after that date, verifying the association's registration status is the first step. The Secretary of State's [Property Owners' Associations Division](https://sos.ga.gov/page/georgia-property-owners-associations-division-faq) will maintain the public registry.

What SB 406 does not do is add the rental protection that POAA owners have. If you were hoping the 2026 legislative session corrected the grandfathering asymmetry between condo and POAA communities, it did not. Condo rental investors in Georgia still carry an amendment risk that their subdivision-HOA counterparts do not.

## CSRA Condo Rental Concentrations: Where the Risk Is Real

The CSRA's condo rental market is more concentrated than many investors realize. The communities where rental restrictions are most likely to be a live issue are:

**Downtown Augusta and Riverfront corridors.** Several high-rise and mixed-use condo developments in the 30901 and 30904 ZIP codes have investor-heavy ownership structures. Augusta's renovation activity around Broad Street and the Medical District has attracted buyers who planned to rent. Some of these communities have adopted or are actively considering rental caps to maintain FHA eligibility. Market rent for a well-positioned 1-bedroom condo in the downtown corridor runs roughly $1,100–$1,500/month depending on finishes and floor.

**Medical District / Augusta University adjacency.** Properties near Augusta University Medical Center and Wellstar MCG Health draw resident physicians, traveling nurses, and rotating health-system staff — strong tenant demand. The same demand has pushed investor-to-owner ratios in some buildings above FHA thresholds, which can trigger association discussions about rental caps.

**Evans and Grovetown condo-adjacent communities.** Columbia County has limited true condominium inventory compared to Augusta, but several townhome and attached-unit communities in [Evans (ZIP 30809)](/evans/) and Grovetown (ZIP 30813) operate under condominium declarations rather than POAA bylaws. The Fort Gordon tenant pipeline makes these attractive, but their governing documents need the same vetting as a traditional condo. Our [Evans rental market guide](/blog/evans-ga-rental-market-investor-guide-2026/) covers submarket context for these Columbia County communities.

**North Augusta, SC.** If your condo unit is in South Carolina, a different statute applies. The South Carolina Horizontal Property Act (S.C. Code § 27-31-10 et seq.) and the South Carolina Homeowners Association Act govern condo communities in North Augusta and Aiken County. South Carolina's rules on rental restrictions differ from Georgia's, and SB 406 does not apply at all on the SC side. ## Evaluating Condo Rental Risk Before You Buy: A Seven-Step Checklist

The due-diligence process for a condo rental purchase in Georgia requires steps that don't apply to a single-family or subdivision home.

1. **Request the complete governing documents.** Declaration, bylaws, and all recorded amendments. The declaration is the governing instrument for rental rights. Do not accept a summary — read the source document.

2. **Identify any current rental restrictions.** Does the declaration prohibit rentals? Does it cap them? Does it require board approval? What is the minimum lease term? Are there tenant registration or move-in fees?

3. **Ask for the current rental ledger.** How many units are currently rented versus owner-occupied? If you buy and rent, will you push the community over FHA thresholds? Some associations track this and can tell you; others cannot.

4. **Review the amendment history.** Look for any amendments that added or changed rental provisions. An amendment adopted two years ago that passed by the minimum required vote tells you the community is activist on this issue.

5. **Check the HOA financial health.** An association with underfunded reserves or ongoing special-assessment conversations is under pressure. Financially stressed associations are more likely to pursue measures — including rental caps — that they believe protect property values.

6. **Talk to current investor-owners.** If other units in the building are rented, the current investor-owners will tell you whether the association has been discussing rental restrictions. This intelligence is not in the documents.

7. **Confirm SB 406 registration status (effective January 1, 2027).** After that date, verify the association is registered with the Georgia Secretary of State. An unregistered association loses the ability to collect fines and fees.

If your analysis shows genuine uncertainty about rental rights, a Georgia real estate attorney can review the specific declaration language before you close. The legal review cost is a fraction of the risk of buying an unrentable unit.

## If You Already Own a CSRA Condo Unit: Your Practical Options

If you already own a unit in a community that has adopted or is considering rental restrictions, your options depend on the specific language and timing.

**When a restriction has already passed:** Review the amendment to understand exactly what it prohibits. Outright prohibitions differ from approval requirements — you may still be able to rent subject to board review. If you have a lease currently in force, that lease contract is not void; your tenant has rights to remain through the lease term. The question is what happens at renewal.

**When a vote is pending:** Participate. As a unit owner, you have voting rights. Georgia law — reinforced by SB 406's meeting-notice requirements — gives you the right to receive notice and attend meetings where votes on governing document amendments occur. A vote requires the threshold specified in the declaration (commonly two-thirds of owners). Your vote matters, and so does organizing other investor-owners.

**When board approval is required:** Understand the criteria. If the declaration specifies objective criteria for approval — credit score thresholds, income requirements, background checks — and your tenant meets them, approval cannot reasonably be denied. If the declaration grants the board discretionary approval, the standard is less clear, but Georgia courts have found that boards cannot act arbitrarily. Document the approval process carefully.

**When a fine arrives for a tenant-caused violation:** The [HOA violation fine responsibility guide](/blog/hoa-violation-tenant-rental-property-georgia-landlord/) explains how Georgia courts allocate fines between owners and tenants, and what lease clauses you need to shift liability to the occupant.

**When you're considering selling:** The presence of rental restrictions in a condo community affects the buyer pool for your unit. Investor buyers will discount a unit in a rental-restricted building; owner-occupant buyers may pay a premium for the same unit in the same building. Know which community type you own in before setting a sale price.

| Feature | POAA Communities | Condo Act Communities |

| Rental restriction authority | Yes (governed by § 44-3-226) | Yes (governed by declaration) |
| Grandfathering for existing owners | Yes — statutory protection under § 44-3-226 | No equivalent statute |
