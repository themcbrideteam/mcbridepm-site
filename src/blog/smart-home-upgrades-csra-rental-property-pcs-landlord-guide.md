## Why Remote Access Control Is Different From a Deadbolt

The standard argument for a smart lock is convenience. That undersells it for PCS landlords.

A traditional deadbolt gives you one or two keys. When your tenant locks themselves out at 7 p.m. on a Sunday, they call you — and if you're at Fort Campbell, the call does nothing except remind you that you need a locksmith number in [Grovetown](/grovetown/). When you turn over the property at lease-end, you pay a licensed locksmith $50–$150 to rekey the lock. If you have a property manager, they coordinate the rekeying, but the cost still hits your owner statement.

A smart lock — specifically one that supports code-based entry without requiring an active Wi-Fi connection at the property — changes all of that.

Your tenant has their own PIN. Your property manager holds a maintenance access code. When the lease ends, you delete the tenant's code from an app and generate a new one for the incoming tenant. The entire transaction takes about 90 seconds with no locksmith involved.

As of November 12, 2026, the four-bedroom homes we have listed in Evans are asking $2,295 to $2,600 a month. At that rent level, a single rekeying costs about 0.2% of gross monthly rent — a nuisance, not a catastrophe. Where the smart lock pays for itself faster is in avoided lockout emergencies, avoided after-hours locksmith dispatches at $200+ per call, and the documentation trail it creates for security and insurance purposes.

When you place your property with [McBride Property Management](/services/), our team holds a separate access code for every managed unit — so vendors can enter for authorized work orders without a lockbox hanging on the door, and without you receiving a call to authorize a code.

## Keyless Smart Locks: What to Buy and What to Skip

Not every smart lock is right for a rental. The critical distinction is whether the lock requires Wi-Fi to function. A Wi-Fi-dependent lock stops working when the router goes offline — which happens during a vacant turn, after a storm takes out power, or when a tenant switches internet providers. That's a problem.

**What to look for in a rental smart lock:**
- Code-based entry that works independently of Wi-Fi (Z-Wave, Bluetooth backup, or standalone keypad)
- Capacity for 30 or more unique user codes
- Auto-lock function so the door locks itself after a set interval
- Low battery alerts pushed to your phone
- Activity log — a timestamped record of every entry

**Common choices among CSRA rental owners:**

| Lock | Wi-Fi Required for Codes | Unique Codes | Typical Street Price | Physical Key Override |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Schlage Encode Plus | Optional (adds app features) | 100 | $200–$280 | Yes |
| Yale Assure Lock 2 | Optional | 250 | $200–$250 | Yes |
| Kwikset SmartCode 955 | No (Z-Wave or standalone) | 30 | $100–$130 | Yes |
| August Smart Lock Pro | Requires Bluetooth/Wi-Fi hub | 100 | $150–$200 | No (keypad add-on required) |

For rentals in Grovetown and Evans where power outages are occasional, the Kwikset SmartCode 955 and Schlage Encode Plus are common choices because they retain code functionality during Wi-Fi outages. The August lock requires an active hub; it's better suited to owner-occupied homes.

**A key override is non-negotiable.** Every rental smart lock should accept a physical key as a backup. If the code fails and the battery dies simultaneously, the tenant needs a way in that doesn't require an emergency locksmith. Keep one physical key with your property manager — that's the [McBride PM standard for all managed properties in the CSRA](/owner-faqs/).

## Smart Thermostats: Protecting Vacant Units and Reducing Tenant Complaints

A vacant CSRA property in December with a thermostat set to 60°F is an invitation for a burst pipe. A property in August set to 90°F because the tenant moved out and no one changed the setting creates conditions for mold growth behind the HVAC air handler.

Smart thermostats solve both problems. They also reduce the most common maintenance complaint from tenants: the "heat/AC isn't working" calls that turn out to be thermostat settings, not equipment failures.

**How smart thermostats work for rentals:**

1. You set a minimum and maximum temperature range (typically 62°F–80°F) that the tenant can't override past.
2. When the unit is vacant between tenancies, you set a setback temperature remotely to protect pipes and prevent mold without running the HVAC around the clock.
3. You receive alerts if the temperature falls outside the allowed range — a signal that the HVAC may be failing before it becomes an emergency repair.

**Georgia law note:** A thermostat is a landlord-supplied fixture. Setting allowable temperature ranges is a property protection measure, not a habitability issue — but be specific in the lease about what range you've programmed so there's no dispute about override rights. If you're unsure how to frame this, the standard management agreement through McBride PM addresses it.

**Cost and tax treatment:** Smart thermostats for rentals typically run $80–$130 for capable models (Google Nest Thermostat, Ecobee SmartThermostat Essential, Honeywell Home T6 Pro). These qualify as five-year personal property for depreciation purposes. Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (P.L. 119-21), 100% bonus depreciation applies to qualifying tangible personal property placed in service in 2026 — confirm with your CPA, and see [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p527.pdf) for the residential rental expense framework.

## Exterior Cameras: What Georgia Law Permits

This is where landlords get in trouble — not by installing cameras, but by installing them in the wrong location.

**The governing statute:** [O.C.G.A. § 16-11-62(2)](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-16/chapter-11/article-3/part-1/section-16-11-62) prohibits using any device to observe, photograph, or record a person's activities in a private place that is out of public view, without that person's consent. A violation is a felony — up to five years in prison and a $10,000 fine under O.C.G.A. § 16-11-69. Evidence from an illegal camera is inadmissible in any Georgia court under O.C.G.A. § 16-11-67, so an illegal camera defeats its own evidentiary purpose.

**The practical rules for CSRA landlords:**

- **Exterior cameras covering the front door, driveway, or yard** are generally permissible. These areas are not "private places out of public view" in the statutory sense.
- **Any interior camera** — even in what you think of as a common area inside the leased unit — is off-limits once a tenant is in occupancy.
- **A doorbell camera that also captures audio** raises a separate question. Georgia is a one-party consent state under O.C.G.A. § 16-11-66(a), but if the audio records conversations between third parties (not you), the analysis becomes complex. The simplest approach: disable audio recording on doorbell cameras, or notify tenants in writing that audio recording is active on exterior cameras and get their written acknowledgment.

**What exterior cameras do for PCS landlords:** An exterior front-door camera lets you verify that a maintenance vendor's work was completed when the invoice says it was, without a site visit. It also documents property condition at tenant move-in and move-out. Store footage in the cloud — most consumer cameras overwrite after 7–30 days.

**Disclosure requirement:** Add a camera addendum to the lease. Specify the camera's location, what it records (video only vs. audio/video), who has access to footage, and how long footage is retained. This is not mandated by Georgia statute as of this writing, but it's the transparent practice our team recommends to every owner we work with. See your [owner FAQ page](/owner-faqs/) for more on McBride PM's standard disclosure approach.

## Leak Sensors: The Highest-Value Upgrade Most PCS Landlords Skip

Smart locks get most of the attention. Leak sensors might deliver the better return.

Water damage claims are among the most expensive repairs in residential rentals. A refrigerator ice-maker supply line that develops a slow drip can go undetected for months, warping the subfloor and promoting mold growth in the wall cavity behind the unit. A compression fitting under a bathroom vanity that starts weeping can saturate the cabinet base and the structural floor below before anyone notices.

**How WiFi leak sensors work:** A small puck-shaped sensor sits on the floor near appliance connections, under sinks, near the water heater, or in the crawlspace access point. When water contacts the sensor's electrodes, it pushes an immediate alert to your phone and to any secondary recipient — your property manager, for example. The alert arrives in seconds, not hours.

**Where to place them in a typical CSRA rental home:**

- Under the kitchen sink (supply line failure point)
- Under each bathroom vanity
- Behind the washing machine (interior laundry connections are a common failure point)
- Near the water heater
- Near the HVAC air handler (condensate drain overflow is a frequent failure in Augusta's humid climate)

**Cost:** Quality WiFi leak sensors run $20–$45 per unit. A five-sensor installation covers a typical 3-bedroom CSRA rental for under $200 total. They require an active home Wi-Fi connection, so installation is most practical in an occupied unit — set them up before the tenant moves in, with the router already active.

**Insurance note:** Some landlord insurance policies include a water damage rider or rate discount for homes with monitored leak detection. Check with your carrier before purchase. The sensors qualify as five-year personal property for the same bonus depreciation treatment described above.

## Smart Smoke and CO Detectors: Meeting Your Georgia Habitability Obligation More Efficiently

Under the Georgia Safe at Home Act (HB 404), landlords must maintain working smoke detectors in rental units. Standard detectors beep when triggered. Smart detectors send a push notification to your phone and your property manager's phone.

If a tenant triggers the alarm cooking and walks away from their phone, you know in real time and can follow up. If the detector goes into alarm at 2 a.m. and the tenant isn't home, your property manager receives the alert and can respond without waiting for a call that may not come until morning.

**Cost and type:** Smart smoke/CO combination detectors run $80–$120 per unit. Combination units are preferred — they cover both Georgia habitability requirements in one device. The upgrade from a standard detector is typically $50–$75 per unit, which is a reasonable cost given what it replaces: a beeping alarm that no one answers when you're in a different time zone.

## What to Disclose in the Lease: A Practical Checklist

The [Georgia Landlord-Tenant Act (O.C.G.A. Title 44, Chapter 7)](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-44/chapter-7/) governs the relationship, and O.C.G.A. § 44-7-11 guarantees quiet enjoyment — meaning tenants have the right to use the property without interference. No Georgia statute specifically addresses smart home device disclosure, but the right approach is to document everything before it becomes a dispute.

**Smart home lease disclosure checklist:**

- [ ] Type of smart lock and that codes are unique per occupant and changed at each tenancy
- [ ] Who holds master/backup access codes (landlord, property manager, emergency contact)
- [ ] Location and type of any exterior cameras
- [ ] Whether cameras record audio; whether tenant consent is provided in writing
- [ ] How footage is stored and who may access it
- [ ] Location of smart thermostat and the programmed allowable temperature range
- [ ] Location of all leak sensors (tenant acknowledgment protects you if one is accidentally unplugged)
- [ ] Location of smart smoke/CO detectors and tenant's obligation not to disable them
- [ ] Procedure for code reset requests — tenant calls property manager, not the device manufacturer

This addendum takes about 30 minutes to draft and prevents a disproportionate number of tenant disputes. The McBride PM management agreement includes technology disclosure provisions — ask about it when you [schedule your free rental analysis](/contact/).

## What to Skip: Technology That Creates More Problems Than It Solves

Not every smart home product belongs in a rental:

**Smart appliances (refrigerators, ovens, dishwashers):** Wi-Fi-connected appliances are complex, proprietary, and expensive to service when they fail. A standard Whirlpool is simpler, cheaper, and easier for the next contractor to work on. Skip them.

**Smart sprinkler systems:** Worth it for high-end properties if the landscaping justifies the investment. For most Columbia County rentals, a standard irrigation timer and clear tenant communication handles it.

**Smart doorbell cameras with audio recording:** Audio creates legal complexity under O.C.G.A. § 16-11-62. If you install a doorbell camera, disable audio recording by default until you have written tenant consent in the lease.

**Interior cameras:** Already addressed — not permitted once a tenant is in occupancy under O.C.G.A. § 16-11-62. No exceptions for "just the entryway."

**Commercial-grade access control systems:** Appropriate for multifamily properties; overkill for a single-family home in Grovetown. The setup cost, subscription fees, and licensing complexity don't pencil for a 3-bedroom rental.
