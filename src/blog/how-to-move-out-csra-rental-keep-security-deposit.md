**How do CSRA tenants move out of a Georgia rental and get their full security deposit back?**
Give written notice within your lease's required timeframe, leave the property in the same condition you received it (minus normal wear and tear), provide your forwarding address at key return, and follow up in writing if your deposit is not returned within Georgia's statutory 30-day deadline.

## When to Give Notice — and What the Notice Must Say

The single most common mistake CSRA tenants make when moving out is giving notice too late — or giving it verbally and assuming that counts.

**For month-to-month tenancies,** [O.C.G.A. § 44-7-7](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-44/chapter-7/article-1/section-44-7-7/) requires 30 days' written notice from either the tenant or landlord to terminate the tenancy. That 30-day clock starts when your landlord *receives* your notice — not when you text it, not when you drop it off, not when you "told them" at the mailbox. Written. Received.

**For fixed-term leases** (the typical 12-month lease in the CSRA), your lease agreement is the governing document. Most Georgia residential leases require 30 to 60 days' written notice before your lease end date. Read your lease carefully: some require notice by a specific date — for instance, "no later than 60 days before the anniversary date" — and if you miss that window, you may automatically renew for another term or convert to month-to-month at a higher rate.

**What your notice should include:**
- Your name as it appears on the lease
- The address of the rental property
- Your intended move-out date
- Your forwarding address (this starts the 30-day deposit return clock — more on that below)
- A request for a move-out inspection, if you want to attend

McBride PM residents can submit notice through AppFolio. You can also use our [30-Day Notice to Vacate form](/pdfs/McBride-PM_30-Day-Notice-to-Vacate.pdf) and send it via certified mail or email to ops@mcbride-pm.com, keeping a copy for yourself. The delivery timestamp matters.

## What Happens at the Move-Out Inspection

Georgia law (O.C.G.A. § 44-7-33) gives both parties the right to document the property's condition at move-out, just as a move-in inspection documents it at the start of the tenancy. The move-in defect list — the written record of what was already damaged or imperfect when you arrived — is the single most important document you have at move-out. If you don't have a copy, request it now from your property manager.

**You have the right to be present at the move-out inspection.** Exercise it. Being present lets you point out anything that was pre-existing, ask questions, and address minor issues on the spot before they become deduction line items. At McBride PM, a move-out walk is typically scheduled within five business days of key return. If you're unable to attend, send a written request for the completed report.

**Document everything yourself before turning in your keys.** Walk every room with your phone and record a dated video. Open cabinets, photograph walls at eye level, check under appliances. This takes 20 minutes and can save you hundreds of dollars if a dispute arises.

What the inspector typically covers:

- Walls and ceilings (holes, stains, scuffs beyond normal use)
- Floors (scratches, stains, carpet damage)
- Appliances (cleanliness, function)
- Bathrooms (mold caused by neglect, broken fixtures)
- HVAC filter (many leases require tenants to change filters)
- Landscaping and exterior (trash removed, no excessive damage)
- All keys, garage openers, and access cards returned

You can download McBride PM's [Move-Out Inspection Checklist](/pdfs/McBride-PM_Move-Out-Inspection-Checklist.pdf) to use as a self-audit before your scheduled walk. Going through it yourself the week before hand-off will surface anything you need to address.

## Normal Wear and Tear vs. Chargeable Damage

The phrase "normal wear and tear" does more legal work in Georgia security deposit disputes than almost any other concept. Georgia courts consistently distinguish between deterioration from ordinary, day-to-day use — which is the landlord's responsibility to repair — and damage resulting from misuse, neglect, or abuse, which the tenant may owe for.

| **Normal Wear and Tear — Landlord's Responsibility** | **Chargeable Damage — Tenant May Owe** |
|------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| Small nail holes from hanging pictures | Large holes from anchors, doorknobs, or accidents |
| Light scuffs on painted walls from furniture | Crayon, marker, or paint applied by tenant |
| Carpet wear along traffic paths | Pet urine stains, burns, or torn carpet |
| Faded or slightly discolored paint after 3+ years | Smoke stains or nicotine yellowing |
| Minor scratches on hardwood floors | Deep gouges or scratches from dragging furniture |
| Loose door handles from normal use | Broken locks or damaged doors from misuse |
| Dusty ceiling fans and light fixtures | Grease buildup on kitchen walls from lack of cleaning |
| A cracked caulk line around a tub | Mold caused by tenant's failure to ventilate bathroom |

The longer your tenancy, the more depreciation a court will factor in. A carpet that was new when you moved in five years ago cannot be charged at full replacement cost — a court would apply a depreciation offset. A carpet that was new when you moved in eight months ago and is now stained is a different conversation.

If you submitted [maintenance requests](/blog/maintenance-request-tenant-guide-csra-rental/) during your tenancy for issues like mold or water intrusion — and the landlord failed to address them — those documented requests are evidence that a problem was pre-existing and unresolved. Keep your AppFolio maintenance history.

## The CSRA Tenant's Move-Out Cleaning Checklist

Most deposit disputes come down to cleaning. The standard Georgia courts apply is: leave the unit in "as good a condition as you received it," accounting for normal wear and tear. If the property was professionally cleaned before you moved in, it generally needs to be professionally cleaned when you leave.

**Complete this list before your inspection date:**

1. **Kitchen:** Clean inside the oven and range hood (including grease filter), wipe down all cabinet faces and interiors, degrease stovetop, clean inside microwave and dishwasher, wipe refrigerator interior including shelves and freezer, clean sink drain.
2. **Bathrooms:** Scrub toilets, tubs, shower tiles, and grout. Wipe down vanity and mirror. Remove any soap scum or buildup. Clean exhaust fan cover.
3. **All rooms:** Vacuum carpet or sweep/mop hard floors. Wipe baseboards and windowsills. Dust ceiling fans, light fixtures, and window blinds. Clean inside all closets.
4. **Walls:** Address any marks or stains with a magic eraser or touch-up paint (if you have the original can or know the color). Don't repaint an entire wall unless you made a major change.
5. **Windows and sliding doors:** Clean glass inside and out. Check that tracks are clear.
6. **HVAC filter:** Replace the filter. This is specified in most CSRA leases as a tenant responsibility, and a clogged filter at move-out is an easy deduction to avoid.
7. **Garage, porch, and exterior:** Remove all personal items and trash. Leave landscaping in the same condition it was in when you arrived.
8. **Final walkthrough:** Go room by room with your phone to document the finished state on video.

If you have pets, give extra attention to odor. Landlords can deduct for professional pet-odor treatment if it's documented as necessary beyond normal cleaning.

## The Security Deposit Return Timeline Under Georgia Law

Once you've handed in your keys and provided your forwarding address, Georgia's statutory clock starts.

**[O.C.G.A. § 44-7-34](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-44/chapter-7/article-2/section-44-7-34/)** gives your landlord 30 days from the later of two dates: (1) the date you actually vacate the property, or (2) the date the landlord receives your new mailing address. This means your forwarding address matters — give it in writing at key return, not after the fact. If you don't provide one, the landlord has a legal excuse to delay.

Within that 30-day window, the landlord must do one of two things:

1. **Return the full deposit** with no deductions.
2. **Return the remaining balance** along with a written, itemized list of all deductions, including the specific reason and dollar amount for each.

A statement that just says "cleaning: $150" is technically valid. A statement that says "deposit forfeited per lease" is not — the landlord must itemize. If you receive an itemized list and disagree with any deduction, you have the right to dispute it in writing and, if unresolved, in magistrate court.

The [Georgia Department of Community Affairs (DCA) Landlord-Tenant Handbook](https://dca.ga.gov/safe-affordable-housing/georgia-rental-laws) is a free state resource that explains your rights in plain language and is worth bookmarking.

## If You Dispute a Deduction

First, respond in writing. Send an email or certified letter to your landlord or property manager stating which specific deductions you dispute and why. Include your evidence: photos from move-in, photos from move-out, your self-inspection video, and any maintenance requests showing a pre-existing issue. Keep this professional and factual — courts respond to documentation, not frustration.

**If the landlord does not respond or resolve the dispute**, you can file in magistrate court. In Georgia, both Richmond County (Augusta) and Columbia County (Evans/Grovetown) magistrate courts handle small claims. Filing costs roughly $50–$75 and does not require an attorney.

**Under [O.C.G.A. § 44-7-35](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-44/chapter-7/article-2/section-44-7-35/),** if a Georgia court finds that a landlord willfully refused to return a deposit — not just made a mistake, but acted in bad faith — the tenant can recover the full deposit **plus up to three times the deposit amount** as a penalty. This is a real deterrent that makes Georgia's deposit law meaningful.

Before filing, pull together:
- Your original lease
- The move-in defect list you and the landlord signed
- Your move-out photos and video
- The itemized deduction notice (if any)
- Any maintenance request history
- All written communications (email, AppFolio messages)

For a deeper dive on the magistrate court process and what "bad faith" actually means under Georgia case law, see our guide on [getting your security deposit back in Georgia](/blog/georgia-security-deposit-return-csra-tenant-guide/).

## Final Steps Before and After Move-Out

A few logistical items that tenants often miss:

**Utilities:** Coordinate utility transfer dates to ensure utilities remain on through your final inspection date and are not disconnected prematurely (landlords need them on to inspect appliances and HVAC). Transfer or cancel service effective the day after your move-out date.

**Mail forwarding:** Set up USPS mail forwarding at [usps.com](https://www.usps.com/manage/forward.htm) before your move-out date. This takes a few days to activate — don't leave it until the last minute.

**Keys and access devices:** Return every key, fob, and garage opener you received at move-in. Missing items typically result in a locksmith charge deducted from your deposit. If you've lost a key, let your property manager know before move-out so you're not surprised.

**Forwarding address, in writing:** As noted above, provide your new mailing address at key return — in writing, either via email or a signed form. This date triggers the 30-day clock under O.C.G.A. § 44-7-34. Don't assume the landlord remembers the address you have on file from your application.

**Renters insurance:** If you carried renters insurance (as required by most McBride PM leases and recommended regardless), notify your insurer of your move-out date. Your policy covers personal property through the end of the policy period, but you'll want to transfer coverage to your new address. For a refresher on what renters insurance covers, see [Georgia Renters Insurance: What It Covers and Why It Matters](/blog/renters-insurance-georgia-what-it-covers/).
