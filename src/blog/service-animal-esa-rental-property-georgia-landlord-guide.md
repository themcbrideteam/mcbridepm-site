You own a rental in [Evans](https://mcbride-pm.com/evans/) or Grovetown. Your lease says "no pets." A new applicant submits an application and a letter from a therapist stating they need an emotional support dog for anxiety. What do you have to do?

This question has gotten materially more complicated in 2026. On May 22, HUD's Office of Fair Housing and Equal Opportunity issued a memorandum that reversed thirteen years of federal guidance on assistance animals — guidance that had given untrained emotional support animals near-automatic accommodation status in housing. Under the new standard, HUD will only pursue Fair Housing Act complaints involving animals that are individually trained to perform disability-related tasks: the same bar the Americans with Disabilities Act uses for service dogs in public spaces.

That sounds like a meaningful change for landlords. It is — but with real limits. The Fair Housing Act as a statute hasn't changed. Courts interpret it independently of HUD enforcement priorities. And Georgia's own Fair Housing Law, enforced by the Georgia Commission on Equal Opportunity, still mirrors the federal floor. What follows is a plain-language breakdown of where things stand today, what you can request, when you can say no, and how to handle every accommodation request without creating Fair Housing exposure.

## The Legal Framework: What You're Actually Dealing With

The foundational rule is this: under 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f) — the Fair Housing Act — it is unlawful to refuse to make reasonable accommodations for a person with a disability when that accommodation is necessary to give them equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling. Assistance animals fall under this provision as a reasonable accommodation, not as a pet.

Georgia's [Fair Housing Law (O.C.G.A. §§ 8-3-200 through 8-3-223)](https://gceo.georgia.gov/fair-housing-division) is the state-level counterpart, enforced by the Georgia Commission on Equal Opportunity. It mirrors the federal standard — Georgia has not passed any standalone ESA statute that either expands or restricts the federal floor. That means HUD's enforcement posture directly affects how the state agency acts on complaints, because both levels of law track the same underlying statute.

Two federal laws are relevant and they work differently:

- **The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)** governs public accommodations — businesses, restaurants, hotels, transit. It covers only dogs (and in limited cases, miniature horses) that are [individually trained to perform disability-related tasks](https://adata.org/guide/service-animals-and-emotional-support-animals/). A landlord is not a public accommodation, so the ADA does not directly govern tenant requests.

- **The Fair Housing Act (FHA)** governs housing. It has historically covered a broader category of assistance animals — including untrained emotional support animals — as a reasonable accommodation for disability. This is the law that applies to your rental property.

The distinction between those two laws is the core of what changed in 2026.

## What Happened on May 22, 2026: The HUD Enforcement Shift

For over a decade, [HUD's FHEO-2020-01 guidance](https://www.hud.gov/sites/default/files/hudclips/documents/AS-Trainor-Enforcement-Guidance-Assessing-Requests-for-the-use-of-an-animal-as-a-reasonable-accommodation-under-the-fair-housing-act.pdf) treated two distinct categories of assistance animals as covered by the Fair Housing Act: (1) trained service animals, and (2) support animals — including untrained emotional support animals — that provided emotional or therapeutic support related to a person's disability.

Under that framework, a tenant with a letter from a licensed mental health professional could effectively require you to accommodate an untrained ESA, and your options to push back were narrow. Online ESA certificates were a documented problem — HUD's own 2020 guidance tried to address fraudulent letters from "internet therapists" with no real provider-patient relationship, but enforcement was inconsistent.

On May 22, 2026, HUD issued a new enforcement memorandum that rescinds both FHEO-2013-01 and FHEO-2020-01 in their entirety. The new standard: HUD's Office of Fair Housing and Equal Opportunity will find reasonable cause of a Fair Housing Act violation only in cases involving animals that are individually trained to provide disability-related assistance. In practice, that means:

- **Trained service animals** → HUD enforcement presumptively applies; denying a qualified request remains a Fair Housing violation under HUD's enforcement framework.
- **Untrained emotional support animals** → HUD will not pursue a Fair Housing complaint on behalf of a tenant whose untrained ESA was denied. The memorandum cites Executive Order 14219, which directed agencies to prioritize enforcement based on the best reading of the statute and which it reads as narrowing FHA's accommodation mandate to trained animals.

**What this change doesn't do.** The FHA as a statute is not amended by a HUD memorandum. Congress would need to pass new law to change 42 U.S.C. § 3604. HUD's enforcement posture is not binding on federal courts — a tenant denied an ESA accommodation can still file a private Fair Housing lawsuit, and a judge may independently find that the FHA requires accommodation of untrained ESAs. State courts applying Georgia's Fair Housing Law have their own interpretive latitude. Legal commentary published after the memo is divided on how durable the shift will prove to be.

The practical implication for CSRA landlords: you have more latitude to push back on untrained ESA requests, document your reasoning, and in some cases deny them — but a blanket "no ESAs" policy still creates meaningful legal exposure in private litigation. Case-by-case evaluation remains the right protocol.

## Service Animals vs. Emotional Support Animals: The Key Differences

Understanding which category applies to a specific request determines how you respond and what you can ask.

| Factor | Trained Service Animal | Emotional Support Animal |
|---|---|---|
| Training requirement | Must be individually trained to perform a specific task related to a disability | No formal training required under FHA; under 2026 HUD memo, HUD won't pursue complaints for untrained animals |
| Species covered | Dogs (miniature horses under ADA public context; FHA is broader in theory) | Any domesticated animal under FHA framework |
| Questions you may ask | (1) Is it required because of a disability? (2) What task has it been trained to perform? | You may request documentation of disability and disability-related need from a licensed healthcare professional |
| HUD enforcement if denied | Presumptively enforceable under Fair Housing Act | Post-May 2026: HUD will not pursue; private lawsuit still possible |
| Pet deposit / pet fee | Cannot charge | Cannot charge |
| Required in "no pets" property | Yes, with valid documentation | Must evaluate case-by-case — cannot categorically refuse |
| Documentation needed | Typically not, unless disability is not obvious | Written documentation from licensed healthcare provider |

The distinction matters most at the documentation step. For a service animal — a dog trained to detect blood sugar drops for a diabetic tenant, for example — you cannot demand verification of training credentials, registration papers, or medical records. You ask two questions. If the tenant answers them credibly, you accommodate.

For an untrained ESA, the documentation standard is where you have more room to scrutinize. A vague letter from an online "ESA certification service" with no evidence of a real clinical relationship is not reliable documentation. A letter from a psychiatrist, psychologist, or licensed clinical social worker who has an actual therapeutic relationship with the tenant is.

## What Documentation You Can — and Can't — Require

This is where most accidental Fair Housing violations originate. A landlord who demands too much — or the wrong things — exposes themselves to complaints as much as one who doesn't engage with the process at all. Our [tenant screening standards](https://mcbride-pm.com/pdfs/McBride-PM_Tenant-Screening-Standards.pdf) document how McBride PM approaches this for every managed property.

**What you CAN do when a disability is not obvious:**

1. Ask whether the animal is required because of a disability (yes/no)
2. Ask what work or task the animal performs, or what disability-related need it addresses
3. Request written documentation from a licensed healthcare professional confirming the tenant has a disability and that the animal is part of their treatment or disability accommodation
4. Evaluate whether the letter comes from a provider with a genuine, ongoing relationship with the tenant
5. Consider the specific animal — its size, species, behavior history — when evaluating whether accommodation is reasonable
6. Set a reasonable response deadline (typically 10 business days) for documentation

**What you CANNOT do:**

- Require the animal to be registered, certified, or trained in any formal program
- Require the tenant to use a specific form or go through a third-party approval service
- Accept or reject documentation solely based on the format it's submitted in
- Charge any form of pet deposit, pet fee, or pet surcharge for an approved assistance animal
- Require the tenant to carry special liability insurance for the assistance animal
- Enforce your "no pets" clause against an approved assistance animal
- Refuse to process the accommodation request because you personally disagree with the ESA system

## When You Can Legally Deny an Assistance Animal Request

Landlords are not required to accommodate every assistance animal request, trained or untrained. Three grounds remain valid under the Fair Housing Act — and none of them is "I just don't allow animals":

**1. Direct threat.** If the specific animal poses a genuine, documented direct threat to the health or safety of other residents or property, and that threat cannot be reduced to an acceptable level through reasonable conditions, you may deny. The threat must be real and individualized — not based on breed, species assumptions, or generalized fear. A pit bull owned by a tenant with no history of aggression incidents is not a "direct threat." An animal with a documented bite history toward other residents may be.

**2. Significant property damage.** If there is documented evidence that the specific animal causes significant property damage that cannot be addressed through normal security deposits or repair processes, you may factor that in. Again, this must be specific to the animal, not a general assumption about the species.

**3. Insufficient or fraudulent documentation.** If the tenant cannot or will not provide adequate documentation from a licensed healthcare professional — or if the documentation is clearly fraudulent (a letter from an "online ESA registry" with no real clinical relationship, for example) — you have grounds to deny and document your reasoning. The 2026 HUD guidance shift gives you more latitude here for untrained ESA requests specifically.

**What is not a valid denial basis:**

- Your lease says "no pets"
- Your HOA restricts animals
- Other tenants might be allergic (you must evaluate, not categorically deny)
- The animal is a breed you personally don't like
- You've had bad experiences with animals in other units
- You simply don't believe in ESAs

Every denial must be documented. If you deny an untrained ESA request, write down the specific grounds — insufficient documentation, direct-threat evidence, or significant damage risk — and retain those records.

## The No-Pet-Fee Rule: What It Means for Your Budget

This rule is one of the most consistent points of confusion for CSRA owners who are new to managing rentals. It has not changed under the 2026 HUD shift.

You cannot charge a pet deposit, pet fee, monthly pet surcharge, or any other animal-related fee for an approved assistance animal. Period. This applies whether the animal is a trained service dog or an ESA — the moment you approve an accommodation request, that animal is no longer classified as a "pet" under Fair Housing law.

What you can do: if an approved assistance animal actually causes damage beyond normal wear and tear — tears up a carpet, destroys a door frame — you can deduct that from the security deposit, just as you would for any other tenant-caused damage. The no-fee rule does not insulate a tenant from paying for specific, documented damage their animal caused.

If you currently charge a pet deposit for regular pets (which is normal practice), you need a clear written policy that distinguishes pet deposits from assistance animal accommodation — and that policy cannot condition an assistance animal approval on paying any form of animal-related fee.

Review your [lease agreement clauses](https://mcbride-pm.com/blog/georgia-lease-agreement-clauses-csra-landlords-2026/) to confirm this distinction is clearly drafted. Ambiguous language is where unnecessary liability lives.

## How to Handle Every Request Without Creating Liability

Consistency is your best protection. A landlord who approves some ESA requests and denies others without documented reasoning creates fair housing exposure through disparate treatment. Here's the process McBride PM follows for every accommodation request:

**Step 1: Get the request in writing.** Whether it comes by email, text, or in person, document it immediately — date, animal type, and what accommodation is being requested.

**Step 2: Determine if documentation is needed.** If the disability and the animal's purpose are obvious (a service dog for a visually impaired tenant), do not demand paperwork. If neither is obvious, proceed to documentation request.

**Step 3: Request documentation with a specific deadline.** Send a written request to the tenant asking for documentation from a licensed healthcare professional. Give 10 business days.

**Step 4: Evaluate the documentation.** Look for a real provider name, license type, license number, and specific language connecting the tenant's disability to the animal's role. An online ESA registry letter with a generic signature is not adequate.

**Step 5: Assess the specific animal.** Is there any documented history of aggression, damage, or threat? What is the animal's size relative to the unit?

**Step 6: Respond in writing.** Approve, request more information, or deny — in writing, with specific reasoning. File the response with the tenant's records. For our owners, this lives in the AppFolio file alongside the lease.

This process applies whether you're managing a 1,200-square-foot ranch home in Grovetown or a three-unit in Martinez. The consistency of the documentation trail is what protects you if a complaint is ever filed with HUD, the Georgia Commission on Equal Opportunity, or a private plaintiff's attorney.
