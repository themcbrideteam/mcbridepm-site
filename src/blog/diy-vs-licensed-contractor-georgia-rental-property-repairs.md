**When does a repair at your Georgia rental property require a licensed contractor?**
Georgia law requires a licensed general contractor for any project where combined materials and labor reach $2,500 or more (O.C.G.A. § 43-41). Electrical, plumbing, HVAC, and low-voltage work require a licensed specialist regardless of cost. Using unlicensed labor above these thresholds creates criminal liability for the contractor and personal liability exposure for you as the property owner.

The repair bid comes back at $2,800 to replace a water heater and fix two bathroom fixtures. A neighbor offers to do it for $900, cash, no paperwork. The math feels obvious. But in Georgia, that decision—made dozens of times a day by CSRA landlords—carries consequences that don't show up until months or years later, when a tenant files a habitability complaint or a water heater fails and someone gets hurt.

Georgia has one of the clearer contractor licensing frameworks in the Southeast. The rules aren't complex, but they're specific—and they intersect with the state's Safe at Home Act in ways that matter for every residential landlord in the Augusta area, whether you own one property in Evans or five across Richmond and Columbia counties.

This post walks through the legal framework, the specific trades where licensing is non-negotiable, how the Safe at Home Act changed the stakes for repair decisions, and a practical decision checklist so you can evaluate any repair before you schedule it.

## Georgia's $2,500 Rule: The Threshold That Changes Everything

Under O.C.G.A. § 43-41, any general construction project where the total cost of materials and labor reaches $2,500 or more requires the contractor to hold a valid Georgia residential or general contractor license, issued by the [State Licensing Board for Residential and General Contractors](https://sos.ga.gov/state-licensing-board-residential-and-commercial-general-contractors) through the Georgia Secretary of State.

That threshold trips up a lot of first-time landlords and accidental landlords who inherited or converted a property. The instinct is to call someone who does handyman work on the side and move on. Below $2,500, that's legally permissible for general repairs. At or above it, that person is operating as an unlicensed general contractor under Georgia law—which is a criminal misdemeanor—and the liability for their work stays with you.

A few important clarifications on how the threshold works:

- **Combined project cost, not a single invoice.** If a repair is expected to cost $4,500 and you split it into two $2,249 invoices for the same unlicensed person, Georgia law treats it as a single project. The split doesn't create a workaround—it creates additional exposure.
- **It applies to the contractor, not only to you.** The unlicensed contractor committing the misdemeanor is the primary violator, but hiring an unlicensed contractor for above-threshold work means you've created a situation where liability for the completed work rests entirely with you as the property owner.
- **The threshold doesn't touch specialty trades.** The $2,500 rule governs general construction work. For electrical, plumbing, HVAC, and low-voltage work, a different rule applies—and it has no dollar threshold.

For routine handyman work clearly under the threshold—rehanging a door, replacing a broken tile, patching drywall, swapping out hardware—unlicensed labor is legal in Georgia. Plenty of CSRA landlords do minor cosmetic work themselves. The threshold simply means you need to know when you've crossed into territory that requires someone with a license.

## Four Specialty Trades That Always Require a License

For specialty trades, there is no dollar threshold. O.C.G.A. § 43-14 governs four categories through Georgia's Construction Industry Licensing Board: electrical contractors, plumbers, conditioned air contractors (HVAC), and low-voltage contractors. Any work in these categories requires a licensed professional—period.

Here's how that plays out across the most common CSRA rental repairs:

| Trade | License Required? | Common Rental Examples |
|-------|-------------------|------------------------|
| Electrical | Yes, always | Panel work, new circuits, outlet replacement, fixture wiring |
| Plumbing | Yes, always | Pipe repair or replacement, water heater installation, drain work |
| HVAC / Conditioned Air | Yes, always | AC replacement, ductwork, refrigerant handling |
| Low-Voltage | Yes, always | Security systems, structured cabling, AV installation |
| General repair | Only if $2,500+ | Drywall, paint, flooring, doors, windows, tile |

This is where most landlord liability exposure actually lives. Replacing a water heater sounds like simple work—disconnect old unit, connect new one. But in Georgia, water heater installation at a rental property requires a licensed plumber and, in most jurisdictions, a building permit. The work involves gas or electrical connections, pressure relief valves, and code-compliant venting. An unlicensed handyman doing that job has created a documented liability problem that stays invisible until a failure causes property damage or a tenant injury.

The same logic applies to HVAC work. Refrigerant handling requires EPA Section 608 certification in addition to Georgia state licensing. An unlicensed person touching refrigerant lines isn't just violating Georgia contractor law—they're violating federal law. That's not an argument you want to be making to an insurance adjuster after an incident.

For specialty trades specifically, the starting point is always license verification—which takes two minutes through the Georgia Secretary of State's GOALS portal.

## What the Safe at Home Act Means for Your Repair Decisions

Georgia's Safe at Home Act (HB 404), effective July 1, 2024, changed the legal landscape for rental property maintenance more significantly than anything in Georgia landlord-tenant law in the prior decade. Every CSRA landlord needs to understand it before making repair decisions.

The Act establishes a statutory duty of habitability that cannot be waived—not in the lease, not by written agreement, not by the tenant, not by you. Your rental property must provide, at minimum:

- Working plumbing with hot and cold running water
- Functional heating and cooling systems
- Operational electrical systems
- Secure exterior doors and windows with working locks
- A structure free from infestations and health hazards

This isn't a new concept—Georgia law already imposed some of these obligations—but the Safe at Home Act codified them clearly and gave tenants a direct enforcement mechanism. Under O.C.G.A. § 44-7-14, a landlord who fails to make required repairs can be held liable for damages the tenant suffers as a result. The timeframe for repairs is determined by the nature of the problem: a non-working HVAC system in August in the CSRA, where temperatures regularly exceed 95°F, is an emergency. A slow bathroom drain is a maintenance issue.

Where this connects directly to contractor licensing: if you attempt to repair an HVAC system using an unlicensed handyman—even with good intentions—and that "repair" fails or causes injury, the use of unlicensed labor will be directly relevant to your liability exposure in a Safe at Home Act claim. You cannot credibly argue you fulfilled your duty of habitability by having someone without the legal authority to perform the work attempt it.

Our earlier post on [Georgia's Safe at Home Act](/blog/georgia-safe-at-home-act-landlord-guide-augusta-ga/) covers the Act's full scope, including notice requirements and tenant remedies. The key practical point here: using licensed contractors for systems repairs is not just good practice—it's part of what it means to fulfill your statutory obligation.

## Building Permits: The Layer Most CSRA Landlords Skip

Beyond contractor licensing, many repairs also require building permits. This is the step that gets overlooked most often—especially by landlords who are managing repairs themselves and trying to move quickly between a tenant move-out and move-in.

In Columbia County, the Building Standards Department issues permits for work affecting the structure, systems, or habitability of a dwelling. In Richmond County (City of Augusta), similar requirements apply through the Augusta Planning and Development Department. Across the CSRA, the following work categories almost always require a permit:

- Any electrical panel work or new circuit installation
- Water heater replacement
- HVAC unit replacement (indoor air handler or outdoor condenser)
- Structural repairs (load-bearing walls, roof framing)
- Additions or modifications to conditioned square footage
- Plumbing rough-in or main drain replacement

Permits matter for three reasons. First, they require an inspection—meaning a licensed professional confirms the work meets code before it's closed out. Second, they create a documented paper trail. Unpermitted work discovered during a property sale is a disclosure problem and a negotiating chip for buyers. Third, if unpermitted work contributes to an injury or loss, your insurance carrier will ask whether permits were pulled—and "no" can void coverage.

To confirm what requires a permit for a specific repair at your property, contact the Columbia County Building Standards Department at (706) 868-3400, or visit [columbiacountyga.gov/390/Building-Permit-Site-Plan-Review](https://www.columbiacountyga.gov/390/Building-Permit-Site-Plan-Review). For Richmond County properties, the Augusta Planning and Development Department is the right starting point.

## The Liability Trap: What Happens When Unlicensed Work Goes Wrong

Let's be specific about worst-case outcomes, because that's what changes the financial math for most landlords.

O.C.G.A. § 44-7-14 makes clear that a landlord may be held liable for damages a tenant suffers due to the landlord's failure to maintain the property properly. Georgia courts have applied this to situations where a landlord hired an unlicensed contractor to perform a repair that later caused injury.

The argument against you in that scenario: you had a statutory duty to maintain the property. You hired someone without the legal authority to perform the repair. The repair failed. A tenant was injured. You failed your duty of care.

Your [landlord insurance](/blog/landlord-insurance-georgia-rental-property-guide/) is the next layer of complexity. Landlord policies in Georgia cover many categories of damage and liability, but most policies include exclusions or limitations for losses that result from code violations or work performed without required permits. "Faulty workmanship" exclusions are common. Some carriers add explicit language excluding coverage for work that required licensure and didn't have it.

The math reframes itself when you look at it this way. Saving $400 on an unlicensed water heater installation can become $15,000 in legal defense costs and a coverage gap on a $60,000 liability claim. This is not theoretical—it's the kind of situation that property managers see when self-managing landlords discover too late that the shortcut created an uninsured liability.

## A Practical Decision Framework for CSRA Landlords

Use this step-by-step checklist before scheduling any repair at your rental property.

**Step 1: Identify the trade category.**
Is the repair electrical, plumbing, HVAC, or low-voltage? If yes—licensed specialist required, no dollar threshold. Stop here and verify license before anything else.

**Step 2: Estimate total project cost.**
If no specialty trade is involved, add together expected materials and labor. At or above $2,500 → licensed general or residential contractor required.

**Step 3: Check permit requirements.**
Call Columbia County Building Standards at (706) 868-3400 or the relevant permitting authority before scheduling. Confirm whether a permit is required for this specific repair, at this specific property, in this jurisdiction. Rules vary between unincorporated Columbia County, the City of Grovetown, the City of Harlem, and City of Augusta-Richmond County.

**Step 4: Verify contractor licensure.**
Use the Georgia Secretary of State's [GOALS portal at sos.ga.gov](https://sos.ga.gov) to search the contractor's license status, license number, and scope. This takes two minutes and provides documented due diligence.

**Step 5: Document everything.**
Keep the contractor's license number, the permit number (if applicable), and all invoices. This is your paper trail if a tenant makes a habitability complaint or if you're selling the property and a buyer's inspector flags previous work.

## What DIY Is Reasonable for Georgia Landlord-Owners

Georgia law doesn't prohibit a landlord-owner from doing minor work at their own property. The liability analysis shifts when you're doing the work yourself vs. hiring someone else, but there's a reasonable list of tasks most competent property owners handle without professional help:

- Interior and exterior painting
- Replacing light switch covers and outlet covers (not wiring)
- Replacing cabinet hardware, door hardware, and deadbolts
- Cleaning and treating surface mold with appropriate PPE (note: structural or HVAC-related mold typically requires professional remediation—see our post on [mold and moisture at CSRA rentals](/blog/mold-moisture-rental-property-augusta-ga-landlord-guide/))
- Replacing toilet flappers and fill valves (simple valve-only work)
- Patching small drywall holes
- Caulking windows, tubs, and sinks
- Pressure washing driveways and decks
- Replacing landscape lighting (low-voltage, plug-in fixtures only)

What you should not DIY at a rental property, regardless of your skill level: anything inside an electrical panel, water heater installation, AC refrigerant handling, main drain replacement, or any structural repair. The liability exposure if these fail—and a tenant is injured—is not offset by the savings. If you're uncertain whether something qualifies as DIY-appropriate, the right call is to get a licensed opinion.
