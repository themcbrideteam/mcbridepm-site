**What screening criteria should a CSRA landlord use for income, credit, and background checks?**
Georgia sets no state-mandated income ratio or credit floor for rental applications — federal Fair Housing and FCRA rules govern the process. CSRA landlords typically require 2.5–3× monthly rent in gross income, a minimum credit score of 580–620, and no eviction judgments in the past 3–5 years. Documenting those thresholds in writing before advertising the vacancy is the single most effective Fair Housing defense available.

You've owned a rental in Evans or Grovetown for two years with the same tenant the whole time. Now they're leaving in 60 days and you're fielding applications for the first time since you bought the place. Three come in the first week. One has perfect income but a 555 credit score. One has solid credit but self-employment income that's hard to verify quickly. The third looks fine on paper, but a background check turns up a four-year-old conviction.

Which one do you approve? More to the point: how do you make that call without exposing yourself to a Fair Housing complaint from whoever you turn down?

The answer isn't a gut call. It's a written screening policy — established before the first application arrives — that you apply consistently to every person who applies. Here's how to build one that holds up.

## Why Written Screening Criteria Are Your Primary Fair Housing Defense

**Written criteria are your answer to a Fair Housing complaint before it's filed.**

The federal Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. § 3604) prohibits discrimination in rental housing on the basis of race, color, national origin, religion, sex, disability, and familial status. Georgia's Fair Housing Act ([O.C.G.A. §§ 8-3-200 through 8-3-223](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-8/chapter-3/article-4/)) mirrors those protections at the state level. Neither law requires proof of malicious intent. A policy that produces a statistically disproportionate outcome on a protected class can itself be a Fair Housing violation under disparate impact theory — a doctrine the Supreme Court upheld in *Texas Department of Housing v. Inclusive Communities Project* (2015) and that remains current law.

The defense against a disparate impact claim is business justification: your criteria serve a legitimate purpose — ensuring rent payment and protecting the property — and are the least restrictive means of achieving it. A written policy, applied uniformly, is the evidence of that justification. A verbal, case-by-case approach is not.

Mention screening to the [Georgia Association of Realtors](https://garealtor.com/) or to any experienced property manager in the CSRA, and the advice is consistent: write the criteria down, date them, keep a copy, and follow them every time. Our [owner FAQ page](/owner-faqs/) addresses this question specifically, and the [Fair Housing compliance guide for CSRA landlords](/blog/fair-housing-compliance-csra-landlords-2026/) covers the seven federal protected classes and how they apply to screening decisions in detail.

## Income Requirements: The 3× Standard and How to Verify It

**The industry standard for CSRA rentals is verified gross income of 2.5 to 3× monthly rent.** Georgia law sets no mandatory ratio — this is a business-judgment threshold that reflects a realistic relationship between income and the ability to pay rent reliably over the lease term.

For a 3-bedroom home in Grovetown or Martinez at $1,500/month, that means looking for verified gross income of $3,750–$4,500/month. For a 2-bedroom in Augusta at $1,200/month, the threshold lands at $3,000–$3,600/month. The average rent across the Augusta area was approximately $1,242/month as of May 2026, according to [Point2Homes market data](https://www.point2homes.com/US/Average-Rent/GA/Augusta.html) — so a 3× standard on an average unit means requiring about $3,726/month in gross income.

The multiple matters less than the consistency. If you set 2.5× as your threshold and document it, you can apply it uniformly and defend it. If you apply 3× to some applicants and 2× to others without a documented reason, you have created a Fair Housing exposure regardless of intent.

**Acceptable income documentation — list these in your policy:**
- Last two pay stubs (W-2 employment)
- Signed offer letter with start date, for applicants beginning a new job
- Last two years of federal tax returns (self-employed applicants)
- Award letters for Social Security, VA disability, or pension income
- For military applicants: current Leave and Earnings Statement (LES), which covers base pay, BAH, and BAS in one document
- Last two months of bank statements as a supplement — not a substitute — for self-employed income verification

Whatever you accept for one applicant in a given situation, you must accept for every applicant in the same situation. That uniformity is the policy in practice.

## Credit Score Standards: Setting a Defensible Floor with Conditional Tiers

**A credit floor around 580–620 is typical for CSRA rental properties.** Above that threshold, applicants generally qualify without conditions. Below it, a tiered policy — requiring a larger security deposit or a qualified co-signer — gives you flexibility while keeping the criteria consistent and defensible.

Here's how a written credit tier structure typically looks for a mid-range CSRA rental:

| Credit Score Range | Standard Qualification | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 680 and above | Qualifies without conditions | Standard approval |
| 620–679 | Qualifies without conditions | Additional rental history review recommended |
| 580–619 | Conditional approval | Additional deposit or qualified co-signer required |
| 550–579 | Conditional approval | Co-signer with credit 680+ required |
| Below 550 | Does not qualify | |
| No credit file (thin file) | Conditional | Larger deposit plus two landlord references required |

Every applicant who falls within a tier receives the same offer under that tier. You cannot offer conditional approval to one 590-score applicant and deny another with the same score without documented reason — that kind of inconsistency is what generates complaints.

Beyond the raw score, look at what's behind it:

- Recent collections or judgments from prior landlords are a stronger disqualifier than old medical debt, which is often outside the applicant's control
- An eviction judgment appearing in the credit file should trigger your rental history criteria (see below)
- A bankruptcy discharged two or more years ago with a clean payment history since is a meaningfully different risk profile than active, recent collection activity


## Rental History: Evictions, Prior Landlord References, and Red Flags

**A single eviction judgment within the past 3–5 years is the most common disqualifying factor in CSRA tenant screening.** An eviction doesn't just signal a prior problem — it signals a process. A court ruled, which means the situation reached the point where no informal resolution worked.

Your policy should specify:

- **Eviction lookback period:** 3 or 5 years is defensible and common. A longer lookback isn't legally required and may create unnecessary exposure if challenged.
- **Prior unpaid rent:** Any unsatisfied money judgment to a prior landlord is a strong disqualifier regardless of when it occurred.
- **Prior lease violations:** Relevant, but needs to be verified — not assumed from a brief reference call. Document what you heard, from whom, and on what date.

Prior landlord references should cover the two most recent rental residences. What to ask:

1. Did the tenant pay rent on time, consistently?
2. Did the tenant give proper notice before vacating?
3. Was the unit left in acceptable condition?
4. Would you rent to this person again?

A "no" to question four without further explanation is still useful data. A "I'd prefer not to comment" is informative in its own way. Document both. ## Criminal Background Checks: The Post-Withdrawal Landscape

**Georgia places no statutory restriction on landlords reviewing criminal history during tenant screening.** That framework was unchanged by HUD's late-2025 action, which removed guidance rather than statute.

Here's what changed: HUD Secretary Scott Turner formally withdrew the agency's 2016 criminal screening guidance — which had discouraged blanket criminal bans — in November 2025. The Federal Register formalized those withdrawals in notices published April 6 and July 17, 2026, according to reporting by the [Fair Housing NC newsletter](https://www.fairhousingnc.org/newsletter/new-hud-guidance-guidance-on-the-applicaiton-of-the-fair-housing-act-to-the-screening-of-applicants-for-rental-housing/) on the prior HUD framework. The withdrawn guidance is no longer enforceable HUD policy.

What did not change: the Fair Housing Act itself still prohibits policies that produce unjustified disparate impacts on protected classes under *Inclusive Communities*. The difference is that the specific HUD framework defining what counts as "unjustified" no longer exists. Courts and HUD enforcement staff must now work from the statute and prior case law directly — which creates more uncertainty, not less, for landlords who rely on blanket exclusions.

**A more defensible approach:**

- Name the **offense types** that disqualify an applicant — for example, felony convictions involving violence against persons, property destruction, or distribution of controlled substances — rather than "any felony"
- Set a **lookback period** — 7–10 years for felonies; 3–5 years for serious misdemeanors is reasonable
- Exclude **arrests without convictions** from your criteria — an arrest is not evidence of wrongdoing and using it as a disqualifier is legally risky
- Allow for **individual review** when circumstances warrant: a 9-year-old conviction with a demonstrably clean record since represents a different risk than a recent one

Write these criteria into the policy. Apply them consistently. Document the reasoning for any denial that involves criminal history, naming the specific conviction, its date, and how it triggered your stated criteria.

*This is general guidance from a property manager, not legal advice. Consult a Georgia attorney familiar with fair housing law before finalizing your criminal screening criteria.*

## Employment Verification: What to Require and How to Document It

**Employment verification answers a different question than income documentation: is this income likely to continue?**

Pay stubs tell you what someone earned last month. Employment verification tells you whether they're still employed there — a distinction that matters when you're signing a 12-month lease. For W-2 applicants, a brief call to the HR or payroll department works: confirm the applicant's name, status (full-time vs. part-time), and start date. Document the contact's name and title, the date of the call, and what they confirmed.

For self-employed applicants, two years of federal tax returns combined with two months of bank statements showing consistent income deposits is the functional equivalent. Neither document is perfect, but together they make fabrication significantly harder.

**What to verify:**
- Employer name and location
- Employment status (full-time, part-time, contractor)
- Start date (a recent start date is not disqualifying but warrants more scrutiny)
- Current compensation

**What not to ask:**
- Reason for any employment gaps — this touches on medical leave, military service, family leave, and other legally protected activities
- Whether the applicant plans to change jobs, get pregnant, or have children — this is explicitly Fair Housing territory
- Work authorization or immigration status — this is a separate, legally complex area that warrants an attorney's guidance

For active-duty military applicants near Fort Gordon, a current Leave and Earnings Statement (LES) covers both employment and income verification in one document. BAH and BAS both count as income in the 3× calculation — a fact that can make a significant difference for junior enlisted applicants whose base pay alone might not clear the threshold.


## The Adverse Action Notice: Federal Law You Cannot Skip

**The [Fair Credit Reporting Act](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/statutes/fair-credit-reporting-act) (15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.) requires a landlord who denies or changes an applicant's terms based on a consumer report to send an adverse action notice.** This duty is broader than it sounds.

If you approve an applicant but required a larger deposit because of their credit report, that is an adverse action under the FCRA. If you required a co-signer based on a background check result, same requirement. The consumer report does not need to be the only factor — if it played any part in a less-favorable outcome, the notice requirement applies.

**What the notice must include:**
- Name, address, and phone number of the consumer reporting agency that provided the report
- A statement that the reporting agency did not make the denial decision and cannot explain why
- The applicant's right to request a free copy of the report within 60 days
- The applicant's right to dispute inaccurate information directly with the reporting agency

**What the notice does not require:**
- A specific explanation of which screening criteria the applicant failed
- A credit score or numeric breakdown from the report (though providing the score is common practice and gives the applicant useful context)

Keep a copy of every adverse action notice. The FCRA statute of limitations is 2 years from discovery of the violation or 5 years from when it occurred, whichever comes first — so records matter. ## What a Written Screening Policy Must Actually Include

**A screening policy is only useful if it covers every decision point — and only if it existed before the first applicant applied.** Set it before advertising the vacancy, not after a tough call forces you to make it up.

**Written Screening Policy Checklist:**

- [ ] **Gross income threshold** — the specific multiple (e.g., 3× monthly rent) and which income sources count toward that figure
- [ ] **Credit score floor** — the minimum score and documented conditional tiers below it (larger deposit amount, co-signer credit requirements)
- [ ] **Thin-file handling** — what you do when an applicant has no established credit history
- [ ] **Eviction lookback period** — how far back you check for eviction judgments and what constitutes a disqualifier vs. a conditional flag
- [ ] **Prior landlord reference requirements** — how many prior residences, the questions you'll ask, and how you'll document the responses
- [ ] **Criminal background criteria** — offense types that disqualify, lookback period, arrest vs. conviction distinction, process for individual review
- [ ] **Employment verification method** — which documents you'll accept and how you'll confirm current employment status
- [ ] **Pet policy** — if pets are permitted, what weight or breed restrictions apply; our [Pet Addendum form](/pdfs/McBride-PM_Pet-Addendum.pdf) covers CSRA-specific terms
- [ ] **Application fee** — the amount and whether it's refundable (Georgia law does not require refunds, but your policy should state your practice clearly)
- [ ] **Decision timeline** — how long after a complete application you'll provide a decision

Once the criteria are written, store a dated, signed copy in your listing records. When you make a decision on each application, log which criteria the applicant met and which they didn't — a one-page decision log per application is the standard that holds up in any subsequent review.
