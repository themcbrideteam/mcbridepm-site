Your lease expires January 31st. Sometime in late October, right when the holidays start crowding your calendar, a renewal offer lands in your inbox. The rent is going up $150 a month. The form has a signature line and a deadline two weeks out.

What do you do?

Most CSRA tenants do one of two things: sign without thinking it through, or panic-scroll apartment listings at midnight. Neither is the right move. The better path takes about 30 minutes of thinking and a short written conversation with your property manager — and it starts with understanding what Georgia law actually says about lease renewals and what options are genuinely on the table.

This guide covers everything a renter in the Augusta area needs to know: how renewal timelines work in Georgia, what rent increases are legally permissible, how to negotiate terms you don't love, and what happens if the landlord decides not to renew at all.

## How Lease Renewal Works in Georgia

Lease renewal in Georgia is not automatic. It depends on your lease type and your lease's own language — two leases in the same Grovetown subdivision can have meaningfully different renewal rules.

**Fixed-term leases** — the standard 12-month agreement most CSRA renters sign — end on the date written in the contract. When that date arrives, the tenancy expires unless both parties agree to continue. Some fixed-term leases include an automatic-renewal clause, which extends the lease on the same terms unless one party gives written notice by a specified deadline, typically 30, 60, or 90 days before expiration. Find your lease's "Renewal," "Holdover," or "Notice" section and read it carefully. That language controls your obligations.

**Month-to-month tenancies** (called tenancies at will in Georgia) run indefinitely until either party ends them. Under [O.C.G.A. § 44-7-7](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-44/chapter-7/article-1/section-44-7-7/), a landlord must give at least 60 days' written notice to terminate. You owe 30 days' written notice. Neither party needs a stated reason — the notice itself is sufficient.

**What "holdover" means:** If you stay past your lease end date without signing a renewal and without giving notice, most CSRA leases convert the tenancy to month-to-month on the same terms. This can be useful if you need a few extra weeks, but it also means your landlord can then end the tenancy with 60 days' notice at any point and can propose new rent at the start of each month.

Understanding these three situations — fixed-term, month-to-month, and holdover — tells you which clock you're on and how much time you actually have to make decisions.

## Notice Timelines: What You're Owed and What You Owe

One of the most common sources of confusion is the difference between "my lease expires" and "I have to be out." These are not always the same date, and the gap between them is controlled by Georgia statute and your lease terms.

| Scenario | Landlord's obligation | Your obligation |
|---|---|---|
| Month-to-month / tenancy at will | 60 days' written notice to terminate | 30 days' written notice to vacate |
| Fixed-term lease, no auto-renewal clause | Lease expires on stated date; no additional statutory notice required | Per your lease — typically 30–60 days' advance written notice of non-renewal |
| Fixed-term lease with auto-renewal clause | Must send renewal or non-renewal notice per the clause's deadline | Must give written notice by the lease deadline to avoid automatic renewal |
| Holdover (past expiration, no new agreement signed) | 60 days' written notice once month-to-month begins | 30 days' written notice to vacate |

For most renters in Evans, [Grovetown](/grovetown/), or Martinez, the practical flow looks like this: your property manager sends a renewal offer 60–90 days before expiration. You have a window — often 15–30 days per lease language — to accept, counter, or decline. If you don't respond, your lease may auto-renew. Many standard CSRA leases auto-renew by default, which means silence equals acceptance of whatever terms were offered.

**How to deliver notice:** Georgia doesn't prescribe a delivery method for ordinary renewal or non-renewal notices (unlike dispossessory and eviction notices, which follow stricter service rules). Your lease likely requires written notice. Best practice: email to your property manager and certified mail to the management office. This creates a timestamped record that protects you if there's any later dispute about whether notice was given on time.

If you're unsure how your rights compare to your obligations, the [Georgia tenant rights guide for 2026](/blog/georgia-tenant-rights-2026-csra-renter-guide/) covers the full landlord-tenant framework, including notice requirements across common scenarios.

## Rent Increases at Renewal: What's Legal in Georgia

Georgia has no rent control and no rent stabilization law. Your landlord can propose any rent amount for a renewed lease — no statutory cap, no approval process, no "reasonableness" requirement.

That said, two important limits apply.

**Retaliation is illegal.** Under [O.C.G.A. § 44-7-24](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-44/chapter-7/article-1/section-44-7-24), a landlord may not increase rent, decrease services, or threaten to terminate a tenancy in retaliation for a tenant exercising a legal right — such as reporting a habitability issue, requesting a legally required repair, or organizing with other tenants about conditions. If a significant rent increase or non-renewal follows within three months of you making a written maintenance complaint, that timing can support a retaliation claim. Document every maintenance request you submit, especially if you do it through an [AppFolio tenant portal](/blog/appfolio-tenant-portal-guide-csra-rental/), which timestamps your submissions automatically.

**Discrimination is illegal.** A landlord cannot raise rent, change lease terms, or decline renewal based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, or disability under the federal Fair Housing Act. If you believe a renewal decision was discriminatory, contact [HUD's Office of Fair Housing and Equal Opportunity](https://www.hud.gov/fairhousing).

**Increases apply at the right time.** For a fixed-term lease, a rent increase only takes effect at renewal — the landlord cannot raise your rent mid-lease unless you agree in writing. For month-to-month tenancies, most CSRA landlords and property managers give 30 days' advance written notice of a rent change, though Georgia statute does not set a specific notice period for rent increases on periodic tenancies. Your lease may specify one.

**How does the market look right now?** CSRA rents rose modestly in 2026. [Zumper's mid-2026 data](https://www.zumper.com/rent-research/augusta-ga) shows a median of approximately $1,350/month across Augusta-area rentals, with [Trulia tracking single-family house rents around $1,500 for a 3-bedroom](https://www.trulia.com/average-rent-market-trends/augusta-ga/). [Rentometer's Augusta-Richmond County data](https://www.rentometer.com/average-rent-in/metro/augusta-richmond-county-ga-sc) puts the 3-bedroom house median closer to $1,550, with an average of $1,634. If the proposed increase puts your rent significantly above current market comparables for your area and unit type, you have a reasonable data point to reference in a negotiation.


## How to Negotiate a Lease Renewal

You have more leverage than most tenants realize — especially after 12 months or more of on-time payments and a low-maintenance tenancy. A vacancy costs a landlord real money: marketing time, tenant screening, potential make-ready repairs, and 30–60 days of lost rent during turnover. A renewing tenant who cares for the property and pays on time is worth something, and professional property managers communicate that clearly to their owners.

Here is a practical negotiation framework.

**1. Know what you want before you respond.** Decide in advance whether your goal is to reduce or eliminate the rent increase, ask for specific repairs or improvements, negotiate the lease term length, or simply confirm renewal on current terms. Going into the conversation without a specific ask is the fastest way to end up accepting terms you didn't want.

**2. Research the market first.** Before you counter anything, check what comparable homes or apartments rent for right now in your specific neighborhood. Search Zillow, Apartments.com, or Trulia with filters for your bedroom count and ZIP code — 30809 for Evans, 30813 for Grovetown, 30907 for Martinez. If comparable rents have stayed flat or softened, you have data to support your counter. If comparable rents have risen faster than your proposed increase, that context matters too.

**3. Lead with your track record.** When you respond, don't start with "the increase is too high." Start with the relationship: "We've been here two years, paid on time every month, and have no open maintenance issues. We'd like to continue." That framing positions you as an asset worth retaining before you get to the number. Noah McBride, McBride Property Management's Principal Broker, notes that a written record of zero late payments is the most persuasive document a renewing tenant can reference.

**4. Make a specific, reasonable counter.** If the increase is $150, your counter might be $75, or zero increase in exchange for signing a 24-month term — stability has value to a landlord too. A vague "we can't afford this" is weaker than "comparable 3-bedroom homes in our neighborhood are renting at $X right now — can we meet in the middle at $Y?" Specific proposals get specific responses.

**5. Ask for it in writing.** Any agreed change to renewal terms must be reflected in a signed lease addendum or a formally revised renewal lease. A verbal agreement from a leasing agent does not protect you if something changes — only the signed document does.

**6. Time the conversation correctly.** The best negotiation window is when the landlord sends the renewal offer, not the last week before expiration. Property managers in the CSRA typically send renewal notices 60–90 days out. Respond within 10–14 days of receipt. At that point, the landlord still has time to list and market if you decline — but they haven't yet committed to doing so. That window is when your leverage is highest.

**Other items to negotiate beyond rent:** Repainting or touch-up work before you sign. Appliance upgrades (if one is aging). A cap on mid-term increases. Permission to add a pet under a formal pet addendum. A six-month or 18-month term instead of 12 months if your situation warrants it. Keep the ask list short — prioritize your top two items and don't dilute the conversation.

## When the Landlord Won't Renew: Your Options

Non-renewal — a landlord deciding not to offer a new lease — is legal in Georgia as long as it isn't discriminatory or retaliatory. You are not entitled to an indefinitely renewing lease.

**Check the timing against retaliation.** If the non-renewal comes within 90 days of a habitability complaint, maintenance escalation, or another protected action you took, note the timeline. The retaliation protection under O.C.G.A. § 44-7-24 creates a presumption of retaliation in that window. This is worth raising with a Georgia attorney or a local legal aid organization before you accept the non-renewal and start your move-out planning.

**Confirm the move-out date in writing.** Ask your landlord or property manager to confirm in writing the exact date your tenancy ends and when you need to return keys. This matters for your security deposit clock — Georgia law starts the return period from when you vacate, not from your lease end date.

**Document the condition before you leave.** The single largest source of security deposit disputes in the CSRA is condition-at-move-out. Take a thorough video walkthrough of every room, every surface, and every appliance on your last day. Download [McBride PM's Move-Out Inspection Checklist](/pdfs/McBride-PM_Move-Out-Inspection-Checklist.pdf) as your guide — it covers every category a property manager will check. See also our complete guide to [keeping your security deposit in a Georgia rental](/blog/how-to-move-out-csra-rental-keep-security-deposit/) and the detailed breakdown of [Georgia security deposit return rules](/blog/georgia-security-deposit-return-csra-tenant-guide/).

**Know the security deposit timeline.** Under O.C.G.A. § 44-7-34, your landlord must return your deposit — or send an itemized written list of deductions — within one business day of your vacating. If they miss that deadline, they may forfeit the right to deduct. Keep your forwarding address in writing so there is no dispute about whether they knew where to send it.

**Start your search early.** Rental inventory in the CSRA tightens in fall and winter, with February through April the most competitive leasing window. If you receive a non-renewal notice in November or December, begin your search immediately — don't wait for the holidays to pass. ## Renewing vs. Moving: A Decision Framework

The lease renewal decision isn't just about the rent number. Here's a framework for thinking it through honestly.

| Factor | Reason to renew | Reason to move |
|---|---|---|
| Rent increase | At or below market rate for comparable units | Significantly above comparable rents in your area |
| Property condition | Well-maintained, maintenance requests handled promptly | Recurring unresolved issues, deferred repairs, aging systems |
| Location | Proximity to work, Fort Gordon, the Savannah River Site, or key services is a core need | Location has become less important or your commute has changed |
| Landlord/PM relationship | Transparent communication, professional management, accurate accounting | Poor responsiveness, billing confusion, unclear charge history |
| Life situation | Stability for the next 12 months is valuable | Job change, family change, or flexibility matters more than savings |
| Move costs | High relative to the rent difference | Manageable; the difference in rent justifies the transition over 12 months |
| Lease terms | Terms are fair and clear | Terms have clauses you're uncomfortable with that weren't there before |

If two or more "reason to move" factors are true, run the full math: first month, security deposit, moving costs, and time. Moving is rarely as cheap as the monthly rent difference makes it look. A $100/month increase on a renewal often costs less than the out-of-pocket costs of a move in the first six months alone.

If you're on the fence and considering breaking your current lease rather than waiting for expiration, see our guide to [early lease termination rights in Georgia](/blog/breaking-lease-early-georgia-csra-tenant-guide/) for a clear breakdown of what that path actually costs.

## 30-Day Pre-Decision Checklist

Do these things in the 30 days before your decision deadline:

1. **Re-read your lease.** Locate the renewal, holdover, and notice sections. Note every deadline, and check whether your lease auto-renews without written notice.
2. **Calculate the true cost of moving.** Security deposit (typically one month's rent), moving truck or labor, first month elsewhere, and time off work. Factor all of it.
3. **Pull comparable listings.** Search Zillow, Apartments.com, and Trulia for your bedroom count and ZIP code. Know the going rate before you respond to anything.
4. **Check your payment history.** Log into AppFolio or your tenant portal and confirm zero balances. This is your negotiating credential.
5. **List your asks.** Are there repairs that should have been addressed? Features you'd want? Lease term flexibility you need? Prioritize to your top two items.
6. **Write your response.** Even a one-paragraph email is a written record. State your intent — renewing or not — and any asks, with a clear timeline.
7. **If renewing with changes, wait for the revised document.** Never sign a renewal based on a verbal understanding of a different rent or different terms. Only sign the lease that shows the agreed numbers.
8. **If not renewing, give written notice properly.** Follow your lease's required method and keep a timestamped copy. Certified mail leaves no room for dispute.
