**What's the difference between normal wear and tear and tenant damage in Georgia?**
Under [O.C.G.A. § 44-7-34](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/2020/title-44/chapter-7/article-2/section-44-7-34/), Georgia landlords cannot deduct ordinary wear and tear from a security deposit. Deductible damage requires negligence, carelessness, accident, or abuse by the tenant — not just deterioration from normal use. The distinction determines what you keep from the deposit and what you absorb as the cost of owning a rental property.

## What Georgia Law Actually Says About Wear and Tear

The rule is in [O.C.G.A. § 44-7-34](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/2020/title-44/chapter-7/article-2/section-44-7-34/): a security deposit cannot be retained to cover "ordinary wear and tear which occurred as a result of the use of the premises for the purposes for which the premises were intended, provided that there was no negligence, carelessness, accident, or abuse on the part of the tenant."

Read that carefully. The law doesn't say "if the tenant caused it, you can charge." It says the tenant must have been **negligent, careless, in an accident, or abusive** for the damage to be deductible. Normal use that slowly deteriorates the property is on you.

The practical implication: a carpet that's worn flat in the main traffic path after two years of normal family living is wear and tear. A carpet with a bleach stain — carelessness — is tenant damage. The worn path is your cost as a property owner. The stain is their cost.

This sits inside a framework that also includes:

- **O.C.G.A. § 44-7-33** — requires a signed written inspection at move-in, which establishes the baseline you'll need to prove what was pre-existing versus what the tenant caused
- **O.C.G.A. § 44-7-30.1** — the Safe at Home Act provision (effective July 1, 2024) that caps security deposits at two months' rent for leases entered or renewed on or after that date
- **O.C.G.A. § 44-7-35** — the penalty provision: bad faith failure to return or itemize within the required window exposes you to three times the wrongfully withheld amount plus attorney's fees

The [Nolo overview of Georgia security deposit law](https://www.nolo.com/legal-encyclopedia/georgia-security-deposits-36205.html) summarizes these provisions well. For the official text, check the Georgia General Assembly's code directly.

## Wear and Tear vs. Damage: A Component-by-Component Guide

This is what CSRA landlords actually need: a clear table showing what crosses the line and what doesn't, based on Georgia courts' treatment of the wear-and-tear standard and [HUD's Handbook 4350.3 guidance on the distinction](https://gocodebook.com/library/us/federal/us-hud-handbook-4350-3-rev-1-occupancy/document-package-for-applicant-s-tenant-s-consent-to-the-release-of-information/example-wear-and-tear-versus-damage).

| Component | Normal Wear and Tear (not deductible) | Tenant Damage (deductible) |
|-----------|--------------------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Carpet** | Compressed traffic paths; minor matting; gradual fading | Pet stains; bleach damage; burns; large tears or rips; stains that won't clean |
| **Interior paint** | Fading over 3–5 years; minor smudges from normal use | Unauthorized paint colors; large crayon/marker marks; holes requiring patching |
| **Walls** | Small nail holes from hanging pictures; light scuffs | Fist-sized or larger holes; anchored furniture damage; mold from tenant's failure to ventilate |
| **Hardwood floors** | Minor surface scratches from normal foot traffic; dulling over time | Deep gouges; pet urine staining into the wood; water damage from ignored spills |
| **LVP/vinyl plank** | Minor surface wear; slight discoloration at seams over years | Cracked, peeling, or lifted planks; burns; heavy gouges |
| **Ceramic tile** | Grout discoloration with age; minor chips from normal use | Cracked or broken tiles; grout destroyed by mold from tenant's negligence |
| **Doors** | Normal operation wear on hinges; slight paint wear at handle area | Holes kicked or punched through doors; damaged locks from forced entry attempts |
| **Window blinds** | Light yellowing or slight brittleness with age | Missing or broken slats; bent or destroyed mechanisms |
| **Appliances** | Normal wear on interior surfaces; gradual efficiency loss | Broken knobs; damaged heating elements from improper use; burn damage |
| **Bathroom fixtures** | Mineral deposits from hard water over time | Caulking destroyed by mold from poor tenant cleaning; cracked fixtures |
| **Light fixtures** | Burned-out bulbs; minor dust/oxidation | Broken globes; fixtures physically damaged |
| **Cabinets** | Minor surface wear; slight loosening of hinges | Doors broken off; interior liners destroyed; serious water damage under sink from ignored leaks |

The hardest calls are always in the middle. Light carpet soiling on a 6-year-old carpet that's already earned its useful life? Probably wear and tear. The same soiling on carpet installed 8 months before the tenant moved in? That's worth pursuing.

## The Useful Life Principle: How to Pro-Rate Deductions

Georgia courts don't require landlords to prove they've lost full replacement value when damage is genuine — but they do expect a reasonable relationship between the deduction and the value destroyed. The legal vehicle for this is the **useful life principle**: you can only deduct the value remaining in the damaged item, not its full replacement cost.

The math is straightforward:

> **Deductible amount = (Remaining useful life ÷ Total useful life) × Replacement cost**

A concrete example: Carpet in a bedroom was 4 years old when the tenant moved out. It has a standard useful life of 5 years. The tenant left a large pet stain requiring full replacement at a cost of $1,800.

- Remaining useful life: 5 − 4 = 1 year
- Deductible fraction: 1 ÷ 5 = 20%
- Deductible amount: 20% × $1,800 = **$360**

The landlord absorbs $1,440. That feels harsh — but the alternative is worse: overclaiming, getting challenged in magistrate court, and potentially facing treble damages.

This table shows industry-standard useful life ranges for the components CSRA landlords deal with most. These are not Georgia statutory standards; they are widely accepted guidelines used in landlord-tenant disputes and capital needs assessments, including [HUD guidance on building component useful life](https://www.hud.gov/sites/documents/eul_for_cna_e_tool.pdf).

| Component | Standard Useful Life |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Standard residential carpet | 5–7 years |
| Luxury vinyl plank (LVP) | 15–20 years |
| Ceramic or porcelain tile | 25+ years |
| Hardwood floors | 25+ years |
| Interior paint (standard grade) | 3–5 years |
| Kitchen/bath caulk | 5–7 years |
| Window blinds (standard) | 3–5 years |
| Refrigerator | 10–12 years |
| Dishwasher | 8–10 years |
| Range/oven | 10–15 years |
| Water heater | 8–12 years |
| Exterior paint | 7–10 years |

If your 3-year-old carpets are destroyed by pet urine, you can't invoice the tenant $3,000 when the carpet had only 2 years of useful life remaining on a 5-year schedule. You can deduct $1,200 (2 ÷ 5 × $3,000). That's your legitimate claim.

The practical takeaway: document your component ages at every turnover. When you replace a carpet, note the date and cost in your property records. That date becomes your baseline when the next tenant moves in and potentially causes damage three years later.

## The 30-Day Clock: What Georgia Actually Requires

From the moment you regain possession — which courts generally treat as the date the tenant returns keys or vacates, whichever is later — you have exactly **30 days** to either return the full deposit or send a written itemized statement of deductions.

Under [O.C.G.A. § 44-7-35](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/2020/title-44/chapter-7/article-2/section-44-7-35/), failing to meet this deadline works a **forfeiture of all your rights** to withhold any portion of the deposit or bring an action against the tenant for property damage. Miss the deadline and you owe the entire deposit back — regardless of what the tenant actually did to your property.

A landlord who willfully fails to return the deposit or provide the itemized statement in bad faith is liable for three times the amount wrongfully withheld plus the tenant's reasonable attorney's fees.

**What your itemized statement must include:**

- [ ] The name and forwarding address of the tenant (from your records)
- [ ] A specific description of each item of damage claimed
- [ ] The dollar amount for each line item
- [ ] Supporting documentation reference (photos, receipts, contractor quotes)
- [ ] The remaining deposit returned (or a statement that none remains)
- [ ] Your name and address as the owner or agent
- [ ] Date of the statement and date sent

Send it certified mail with return receipt to the tenant's last known forwarding address, and keep the receipt. If the tenant never gave a forwarding address, documented attempts to reach them protect you from the bad-faith finding.

A vague line like "cleaning: $325" or "repairs: $450" without specifics is not a valid itemized statement. Courts in Columbia County and Richmond County have rejected these; the whole deposit gets returned.

## Writing a Legally Defensible Itemized Statement

Your itemized statement is both a business document and a legal filing. It should read like a property manager wrote it — not like you're arguing.

Here is what a defensible deduction line looks like:

> **Master bedroom carpet replacement** — $420 of $1,800 replacement cost (useful life: 5 years; carpet installed June 2023; lived in life at tenant departure: 2 years; remaining life: 3 years; deductible fraction: 3/5 × $1,800 = $1,080; prior pet deposit applied: $660; remaining deduction from security deposit: $420). Invoice from Regional Flooring, October 29, 2026, attached.

That level of detail won't always be necessary, but on any deduction over $200, it's what wins disputes. Judges at Columbia County Magistrate Court see hundreds of these cases. The ones with math and receipts win; the ones without paperwork lose.

## What Columbia County Magistrate Court Actually Needs

If a tenant disputes your deductions, they can file a small claim in magistrate court. Columbia County's Magistrate Court handles these routinely — Noah McBride, who has been through this process with property owners, describes it as one of the most evidence-dependent proceedings a landlord will face.

What holds up:

- **Signed move-in inspection form** — O.C.G.A. § 44-7-33 requires this. Without it, any damage could be "pre-existing." McBride Property Management's [move-in inspection documentation process](/blog/move-in-inspection-documentation-csra-landlords-georgia/) produces the signed baseline courts need.
- **Timestamped before-and-after photos** — 60+ photos at move-in, the same areas photographed at move-out. Courts routinely ask landlords to point to the specific photo showing the item in dispute.
- **Contractor quotes or invoices** — not your own estimate of what it costs. A written quote from a licensed contractor or an actual invoice from completed work.
- **The itemized statement itself, sent within 30 days** — the certified mail receipt is your proof of compliance.

What doesn't hold up:

- A generic "cleaning fee" without documentation of move-in cleanliness
- Full replacement costs for components with significant age
- Deductions for items that show up in neither the move-in form nor the photos
- An itemized statement emailed the day before the 30-day deadline without proof of delivery

## When to Write Off the Loss and Move On

Not every deduction is worth pursuing. This is a business decision, not a moral one.

If the total legitimate deduction is under $300–$400, do the math: a magistrate court filing fee, your time preparing evidence, the risk that a judge finds one procedural defect and orders you to return the whole deposit, the risk of a bad-faith finding if your documentation is thin — all of these may outweigh the amount you'd recover.

Specific situations where absorbing is often the right call:

- Your move-in documentation is weak (no photos, unsigned form, or no copies provided to tenant)
- The component in question was near the end of its useful life and the pro-rated deduction is small
- The damage is cosmetic and you were planning to repaint or recarpet anyway
- The tenant has indicated they'll dispute, and they know their rights well enough to make the process expensive

The goal of a sound security deposit process isn't to squeeze every dollar out of every departing tenant. It's to have a credible, documented process that accurately compensates you for genuine damage — and to do it on time, every time.
