**What do you need to check before listing a rental property in the CSRA?**
Walk eight systems in order — exterior structure, HVAC, plumbing, electrical, interior finishes, safety devices, locks, and appliances — and fix any habitability issue before the lease is signed. Under Georgia's Safe at Home Act (O.C.G.A. § 44-7-13), the warranty of habitability attaches at lease execution, not move-in day.

You didn't plan to become a landlord. Maybe you inherited a house in Evans when your uncle passed. Maybe a job opportunity in Atlanta came through faster than your Columbia County home sold, and renting it out for two or three years makes more financial sense than taking a loss. Maybe a divorce left one spouse holding a property neither of them planned to manage alone.

Whatever brought you here, you're about to put a home in front of strangers and collect rent on it. The first thing you need to do — before you call a property manager, before you price the rent, before you take a single photo for the listing — is walk the property.

## Why Georgia's Habitability Warranty Changes the Stakes for First-Time Landlords

Before 2024, Georgia landlords had a general duty to maintain rental properties under O.C.G.A. § 44-7-13, but the law's scope was limited and courts interpreted it narrowly. That changed when the [Safe at Home Act (HB 404)](../georgia-safe-at-home-act-landlord-guide-augusta-ga/) took effect July 1, 2024.

The Safe at Home Act added an express warranty of habitability to every Georgia residential lease — meaning every lease is now "deemed to include a provision that the premises is fit for human habitation," per the amended § 44-7-13(b). The warranty is **non-waivable**: any lease clause that asks a tenant to accept the property as-is and release the landlord from habitability obligations is unenforceable under O.C.G.A. § 44-7-2(b).

What that means for a first-time landlord in Evans, Grovetown, or Augusta: the moment your tenant signs the lease, you have legally guaranteed that the home is fit for human habitation. If the HVAC quits on move-in weekend because the capacitor was already failing when you listed, that's your responsibility — not a "wear and tear" issue the tenant has to absorb. The [full legal framework for what Georgia's habitability requirements cover](../georgia-rental-habitability-requirements-csra-landlords-2026/) is worth reading before your first lease, but the operational takeaway is straightforward: find the problems before the lease is signed, not after.

The [National Low Income Housing Coalition](https://nlihc.org/resource/georgia-passes-new-tenant-protection-law-renters-establishing-minimum-habitability), which tracked the bill's passage, describes HB 404 as establishing "minimum habitability standards" for the first time in Georgia's residential rental code — a significant shift from the previous patchwork of local enforcement and implied duties. For landlords, that shift translates into one practical action: a thorough pre-rental walkthrough before every listing.

## System 1: Exterior and Structural

Start outside, work your way in. Structural problems are the most expensive to miss.

**Roof and attic access:** Look for missing or lifted shingles, granule loss in the gutters, and any visible sagging along the ridge line. If the roof is more than 15 years old and you have no service records, budget for a licensed roofer's inspection. A leaking roof triggers both a habitability claim and a mold liability in Georgia once a tenant reports it in writing — and they will. Check the [CSRA Landlord Field Guide](/pdfs/McBride-PM_CSRA-Landlord-Field-Guide.pdf) for approximate roof replacement cost benchmarks by home size and roofing material.

**Gutters and downspouts:** Clogged gutters cause fascia rot, foundation drainage problems, and water intrusion at the sill. Clean them before listing if you're in fall or winter. Confirm downspouts terminate at least four feet from the foundation.

**Foundation and grading:** Walk the perimeter and look for horizontal cracks in masonry (vertical hairline cracks in a brick veneer are common and usually cosmetic; horizontal cracks warrant a structural engineer's eye). Confirm the grade slopes away from the foundation on all sides — negative grading is the leading cause of wet crawl spaces and basements in Columbia County's red clay soil.

**Exterior walls and siding:** Poke any wood trim near the soil line with a pen — soft spots indicate rot and potential structural compromise. Check caulking around windows and doors. If the exterior hasn't been painted in 10+ years, budget for it; a weathered exterior photographs poorly and signals maintenance neglect to prospective tenants.

**Windows and exterior doors:** Every window should open, close, latch, and lock. Broken seals (the "fogging" between double-pane glass) are cosmetic but signal age; a cracked or single-pane window in a bedroom is a habitability concern in winter. Every exterior door needs a working deadbolt. Test the threshold and weatherstripping — an unweatherstripped door shows up in utility bills, and tenants notice.

## System 2: HVAC — The Highest-Stakes Item in a Georgia Rental

The Safe at Home Act's expansion of protected utilities explicitly includes cooling, in addition to heat, light, and water. That means a failed air conditioner in an Augusta summer is now a legal habitability matter, not just an inconvenience.

Before listing, run the system through a full cycle in both heating and cooling modes. Stand in front of every supply register and confirm airflow. Set the thermostat 10 degrees below the current room temperature, let the system run for 15 minutes, and confirm the air coming from the registers is actually cold. Set it 10 degrees above, run the heat, confirm warm air.

**Replace the filter.** A clean filter is the first thing a tenant or property manager will check, and a 3-inch filter caked in dust signals that the system hasn't been maintained. It also sends the wrong message about how the property is managed overall.

**Check the service history.** For a Georgia rental, the HVAC should be professionally serviced at least once a year. If you can't produce records showing service in the last 12 months, schedule a service call before listing — the technician will catch a failing capacitor, low refrigerant, or a cracked heat exchanger before it becomes an after-hours emergency. Columbia County HVAC service calls run $150–$300 for a standard inspection; that's inexpensive insurance against a $400 emergency dispatch in August. For a full breakdown on HVAC responsibilities and costs, the [McBride PM HVAC guide for Augusta landlords](../hvac-csra-rental-property-landlord-guide-augusta-ga/) walks through the numbers in detail.

## System 3: Plumbing

Run every faucet, cold and hot, for at least two to three minutes. Watch the drain for slow flow — a partial clog that takes two weeks to fully stop up will generate a maintenance request on your tenant's third week in the property. Open the cabinet under every sink and check for active drips, water staining on the cabinet floor, or soft spots in the cabinet base.

Flush every toilet. Confirm the tank refills fully and the fill valve shuts off — a running toilet wastes water and, depending on how utility billing is set up, that's either a landlord cost or a tenant frustration. Check the wax seal at the floor for any discoloration or soft spots in the adjacent flooring.

**Water heater:** Find the serial number and decode the manufacturing date. Most water heaters carry a 6–12 year manufacturer warranty; a tank approaching 10–15 years is a replacement candidate, not a list-and-hope item. A water heater that fails after move-in is a habitability issue that will require an emergency response. Check the pressure relief valve — pull the test lever and confirm water flows freely from the discharge pipe. If it doesn't move or drips continuously after the test, the valve needs replacement before listing.

Look for any active water stains on the ceiling below second-floor bathrooms or utility areas. Brown rings mean there was a leak; fresh discoloration means there still is one.

## System 4: Electrical

You don't need to open the panel yourself, but you do need to test every outlet and confirm the panel is functional. A $12 outlet tester from any hardware store plugs directly into each receptacle and shows open ground, reversed polarity, or open neutral — problems that are invisible but significant, particularly in older Richmond County homes.

**GFCI outlets:** Every bathroom, kitchen (within 6 feet of a water source), and garage outlet should be GFCI-protected. Press the TEST button on each one — it should trip and cut power to the outlet. Press RESET and confirm power restores. A dead GFCI that won't reset needs replacement before listing.

**Smoke detectors:** Georgia law requires a working smoke detector on every level of the home and within 10 feet of every bedroom door. Test every unit with the test button. Replace batteries in all units before the lease is signed — this is a 10-minute, $20 task that is also a legal requirement. Carbon monoxide detectors are not required by Georgia law in most residential rentals, but McBride PM installs them as standard practice in any home with gas appliances or an attached garage. It's the right call.

**The panel:** Open the breaker panel and look for signs of heat (discoloration, melted insulation), double-tapped breakers (two wires on a single breaker terminal), or breakers that have been tripped and left off. If the panel is a Federal Pacific Electric Stab-Lok or a Zinsco panel, consult a licensed electrician before listing — both have documented failure rates and can be uninsurable liabilities. The [guide on DIY vs. licensed contractor repairs for Georgia rentals](../diy-vs-licensed-contractor-georgia-rental-property-repairs/) covers which electrical items require a licensed electrician vs. which you can handle yourself.

## System 5: Interior Finishes — What "Rental Ready" Actually Looks Like

Rental-ready doesn't mean magazine-quality. It means clean, functional, and without deficiencies a reasonable tenant would object to at move-in. The practical standard: if you would be comfortable paying rent to live in this condition, it's ready. If you wouldn't, fix it.

**Paint:** Walls should be in a neutral tone — in the CSRA, builders and landlords gravitate toward agreeable gray, accessible beige, or antique white — and without significant scuffs, nail holes larger than a quarter inch, or areas that look clearly patched without feathering. Fresh paint is the highest-ROI rental-prep item you can do. A full interior paint job on a 1,500 sq. ft. home in Columbia County runs $1,800–$3,500 depending on the contractor; it's the single change that most improves listing photos and move-in perception.

**Flooring:** Carpet with significant staining, pet odors, or visible matting at high-traffic areas should be replaced. Even if you plan to disclose it and price accordingly, carpet that smells of pets will reduce your qualified applicant pool substantially, particularly for Fort Gordon military families relocating with PCS orders who want to move in quickly without negotiating repairs. Hardwood and LVP in good condition need a thorough cleaning; note any loose planks or lifting edges in the walkthrough.

**Cabinets and hardware:** Every cabinet door and drawer should function. Loose hinges take five minutes with a screwdriver. A broken drawer slider or a cabinet door that won't close sends a clear signal about the property's maintenance history.

**Appliances:** If you're including appliances, test each one: run the dishwasher through a cycle, run all stove burners and the oven, test the refrigerator's ice maker if it has one. A broken appliance at move-in is a maintenance request on day one and a move-in inspection dispute that follows the tenant's lease through its entire term. If an appliance is at end of life, it's better to remove it and disclose "no appliances included" in the listing than to have it fail under warranty on your first tenant.

## What to Do When You Find Something Serious

You'll find something. Almost every home that hasn't been tenant-occupied in the last 12 months has at least one deferred maintenance item that surfaces on a systematic walkthrough. The question isn't whether — it's what to do with what you find.

**Fix it before listing if:** the item affects habitability (HVAC, plumbing, electrical), it involves water intrusion, it's a safety device (smoke detector, deadbolt), or it will generate a maintenance request within the first 30 days.

**Disclose and price accordingly if:** the item is cosmetic, clearly dated, and accurately described in the listing (older carpet, dated kitchen, single-pane windows in a secondary bedroom). Some P2 landlords are surprised to learn that a cosmetic disclosure doesn't hurt their rental — it filters for tenants who understand what they're getting and prevents move-in-day disputes about condition.

**Get a licensed professional before you decide if:** you find foundation cracks, water staining in multiple ceiling locations, any smell of natural gas, an older electrical panel that feels warm to the touch, or a water heater over 12 years old. These aren't decisions to make on your own — they need a professional opinion before you commit to a listing price or a move-in date.

If a major repair — a roof replacement, HVAC system replacement, or significant structural work — surfaces during your walkthrough, don't panic. For context, Zillow's June 2026 data puts the average rent in the Augusta metro at approximately $1,350 for all property types, with the CSRA market labeled "cool." A rental-ready property in a good Columbia County location commands significantly more than that average. The economics of making the investment in repairs generally hold when the alternative is a vacant property or one generating maintenance costs in year one.

Before you decide what to repair and what to defer, run the numbers. The [Operating Expenses Worksheet](/pdfs/McBride-PM_Operating-Expenses-Worksheet.pdf) covers common CapEx cost ranges for the CSRA, which will help you make an informed repair vs. defer decision rather than guessing.
