**What happens to a Georgia rental property in a divorce?**
Under [O.C.G.A. § 19-5-13](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-19/chapter-5/section-19-5-13/), Georgia divides marital property — including rental properties acquired during the marriage — through equitable distribution, not an automatic 50/50 split. The four paths are: buyout, sale, deferred sale, or temporary co-ownership. Existing tenant leases survive the transfer. Divorce-related property transfers are tax-free under IRC § 1041, but the receiving spouse inherits the accumulated depreciation and future recapture liability.

</div>

Your divorce attorney handles the legal proceedings. Your CPA handles the tax returns. What neither of them typically covers in depth is what this means for the actual rental property — the tenant still paying on the first of every month, the mortgage with both names on it, and the five years of depreciation on your Schedule E that's about to move to someone else's return.

This is the gap most CSRA property owners hit when a marriage ends and a rental property is in the middle of it. You're managing a real legal and financial asset under the pressure of an already difficult situation, and the decisions you make over the next few months have consequences that outlast the divorce itself.

This guide covers the specific mechanics — Georgia law, operational logistics, and the tax picture — in the order you need to address them.

## Is the Rental Property Marital Property in Georgia?

The answer is almost always yes — if you purchased the property during the marriage using any funds earned during the marriage.

Georgia is an **equitable distribution state**, not a community property state. Under [O.C.G.A. § 19-5-13](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-19/chapter-5/section-19-5-13/), a Georgia court divides marital property in a manner that is equitable — fair given all the circumstances — not necessarily equal. An even split is one possible outcome, not the starting point.

**Marital property** in Georgia means property acquired by either spouse during the marriage, regardless of whose name appears on the deed. A rental in Evans that you purchased with joint savings in 2019 is marital property even if only your name is on the title.

**Separate property** is different. It generally includes:

- Property you owned before the marriage
- Property inherited by one spouse specifically (not gifted to both)
- Gifts received by one spouse individually during the marriage
- Proceeds from certain personal injury settlements

The complication arises when separate and marital funds are mixed. If you owned a rental before the marriage, but your spouse contributed to repairs, renovations, or mortgage payments using marital income, a portion of the property's appreciated value may be considered marital — a doctrine Georgia courts call **transmutation or commingling**. This is a fact-specific analysis that requires your attorney to trace the source of funds.

If the rental was originally your primary residence — common among Fort Gordon military families and professionals who relocated for work — the timing of the conversion matters enormously. Equity that built up before the marriage is yours alone; appreciation and debt paydown that accrued during the marriage sits in the marital pool. Getting the exact timeline documented early in the proceedings prevents a muddier negotiation later.

## Your Four Options for Dividing the Rental Property

Once the property is identified as marital (or partially marital), the divorce settlement must address it. There are four standard paths.

| Option | Description | Best when | Immediate tax event? |
|--------|-------------|-----------|----------------------|
| **Buyout** | One spouse pays the other for their equity share and retains the property | One party wants to continue operating it; the other prefers cash or a different asset | No — IRC § 1041 defers gain |
| **Sale + split** | Sell the property, divide net proceeds per the settlement | Neither party wants management responsibility; market timing is acceptable | Possibly, depending on gain |
| **Deferred sale** | Court orders both to hold until a future date (typically lease expiration) | Tenant mid-lease; retaining spouse cannot yet qualify for a refinance | No — triggers at actual sale |
| **Co-ownership** | Both continue owning the rental after divorce | Rarely advisable; creates ongoing conflict between ex-spouses | No — but sustained legal exposure |

The **buyout** is by far the most common resolution for an active rental with a paying tenant. One spouse keeps the property and compensates the other with cash, retirement account balances, equity in a different asset, or a promissory note. This avoids disrupting a current tenancy and preserves the income stream.

A **direct sale** is the cleanest exit if neither party wants ongoing management, but it typically requires waiting for the tenant's lease to expire — or negotiating a mutual early termination — before listing. A forced sale under a compressed divorce timeline rarely achieves maximum price.

The **deferred-sale structure** appears in contested divorces where neither party can execute a buyout immediately. A consent order or marital settlement agreement specifies who manages the property in the interim, how rental income is split, and when the sale must occur. We have managed CSRA properties in this exact structure and can attest that it requires written agreements on every operational decision from the start.

**Co-ownership after divorce** almost always creates more conflict than it resolves. Both names remain on the title, both remain on the mortgage, and any disagreement about a repair, tenant selection, or rent increase becomes a potential legal dispute between ex-spouses.

## The Mortgage Problem: Why a Quitclaim Deed Isn't Enough

This is the most common misunderstanding in divorce property transfers, and it has caused real financial harm to people who believed they had resolved the issue.

A **quitclaim deed** transfers a spouse's ownership interest in the property — it changes the title record. What it does not do is change who owes the mortgage.

If both spouses are on the original loan, both remain legally obligated to the lender regardless of what the divorce decree or quitclaim deed says. The divorce court has authority over the property between the two of you; it cannot unilaterally modify a contract with a third-party lender.

The only way to remove a departing spouse from mortgage liability is for the retaining spouse to **refinance the loan in their own name**. This requires:

1. Qualifying for the new mortgage on their income alone
2. Having sufficient equity in the property (most lenders require at least 20% for an investment property refinance)
3. Completing the closing and recording the new deed before the departing spouse's credit and financial future remain tied to a property they no longer own

If the retaining spouse cannot qualify for a refinance — which happens frequently after a one-income household, a recent career transition, or a military retirement — a deferred sale or outright sale may be the only viable path. Building this reality check into settlement negotiations early prevents a situation where you thought you kept the rental, then discover six months later that your ex's name is still on the mortgage and they want off.

On the CSRA market specifically: rental properties in Columbia County purchased in the 2019–2022 range — particularly in Evans and Grovetown — have appreciated meaningfully, often creating buyout figures in the $40,000–$80,000 range depending on original purchase price and current principal balance. That's a real asset with real negotiating weight.

## What Happens to the Existing Tenant?

The tenant is not a party to the divorce, and Georgia law protects their position.

An existing written lease survives a change of ownership. When the property transfers from joint ownership to one spouse — or to a new entity after the divorce — the new owner is bound by every term of the existing lease: the rent amount, the lease end date, the pet addendum, any special provisions. The tenant cannot be displaced mid-lease simply because the ownership changed. Attempting to remove a paying tenant to facilitate a sale is a separate legal process requiring the proper dispossessory procedure under O.C.G.A. Title 44, Chapter 7.

Three operational steps are required when the property transfers:

**Security deposit transfer.** Under [O.C.G.A. § 44-7-33](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-44/chapter-7/section-44-7-33/), the prior landlord must transfer any security deposit held to the new owner at the time of the ownership transfer. Both parties should document this in writing. Failing to transfer the deposit properly exposes the prior owner to liability for deposit refund claims they may no longer have funds to satisfy — a problem that surfaces two years later when the tenant moves out.

**Tenant notification.** Georgia law requires the landlord to provide tenants with the name and address of the property owner (or their authorized property manager) for rent payment purposes. When ownership transfers, the new owner or their management agent should provide this notice promptly in writing.

**Property management transition.** If the retaining spouse decides to bring in professional management — a reasonable choice when you're newly managing a rental solo after a life transition — the management firm steps in as the authorized agent. This doesn't require the tenant's consent; it's a management relationship, not a change to the lease itself. The tenant continues paying rent and following the existing lease terms.

## The Tax Picture: What the Recipient Inherits

The federal tax treatment of a divorce-related property transfer is governed by **IRC § 1041**. Transfers of property between spouses — or to a former spouse, if the transfer is incident to the divorce — are generally non-recognition events. No gain or loss is recognized at the time of transfer.

**"Incident to the divorce"** has a specific legal meaning under [Temp. Reg. § 1.1041-1T](https://federal-regs.com/title/26/part-1/1.1041-1T/): either the transfer occurs within one year of the divorce becoming final, or it is made pursuant to a divorce or separation instrument and occurs within six years of that date. Transfers within those windows are tax-free — neither spouse pays capital gains on the transfer itself.

The catch is **basis**. The receiving spouse does not get a fresh start on their cost basis. They inherit the transferor's original adjusted basis: the purchase price, plus capital improvements, minus all accumulated depreciation claimed over the years. They also inherit the **unrecaptured Section 1250 gain** — the depreciation recapture component — that the prior owner had built up.

A concrete example: suppose the property was purchased for $200,000 in 2018, has $28,000 of accumulated straight-line depreciation through 2026, and is now worth $290,000. The receiving spouse inherits a $172,000 adjusted basis. When they eventually sell, the IRS will calculate gain against $172,000, not $290,000. The $28,000 of accumulated depreciation is taxed at rates up to 25% under Section 1250 recapture, on top of any capital gain. The deferred tax liability is real.

**Going forward after transfer**, the receiving spouse continues claiming the same annual depreciation deduction on Schedule E — they pick up exactly where the prior owner left off, same depreciable basis, same remaining useful life. No reset. No choice of method. The same applies to any [rental property tax deductions](/blog/rental-property-tax-deductions-georgia-landlords/) already in place (mortgage interest, insurance, management fees, repairs).

Two things to confirm with your CPA before recording the deed:

1. **Georgia Real Estate Transfer Tax.** Transfers made pursuant to a court order — including property divisions in a final divorce decree — are generally exempt under O.C.G.A. § 48-6-21(8). Confirm this exemption applies to your specific transaction before recording.
2. **Mid-year transition on Schedule E.** If ownership changes during the tax year, each spouse reports income and expenses for the portion of the year they held title. Your CPA needs the exact transfer date and supporting documents.

[IRS Publication 504 (Divorced or Separated Individuals)](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p504) and [IRS Publication 527 (Residential Rental Property)](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527) are the primary federal references for this transition. Review both with your CPA.

## Rental Income During the Proceedings

Once a divorce petition is served in Georgia, the **automatic standing order under Ga. Code § 19-1-1** takes effect immediately. It prohibits both spouses from selling, transferring, encumbering, or otherwise disposing of marital property — with an exception for transactions in the **ordinary course of business**.

Collecting monthly rent and paying the mortgage and operating expenses fall within ordinary business operations. The standing order is not a freeze; it's a restraint on extraordinary transfers. Problems arise when one spouse pockets rent without applying it to the mortgage and expenses, or when both spouses disagree about whether a repair should be authorized.

**Best practice while the case is pending:** open a dedicated rental account in both names, direct the tenant to pay into that account, pay all operating expenses from it, and keep records of every transaction. The net income — or deficit — becomes part of the marital accounting. We can set up a management arrangement that holds rent in a transparent, documented account, which simplifies the accounting for both attorneys.

If the rental is in only one spouse's name, that spouse remains the landlord of record and is responsible for operations. The court may consider the rental income stream in the overall equitable distribution analysis — offsetting it against other assets that went to the other spouse.

## Practical Checklist: Handling a Rental Through a Georgia Divorce

Work through these steps in order. Each has dependencies.

1. **Confirm marital vs. separate property** — deed, purchase date, source of funds. Your attorney handles this.
2. **Get an appraisal or BPO** — both parties need a shared, defensible current value.
3. **Pull the lease** — identify the term end date, monthly rent, and security deposit amount. This determines your timeline flexibility.
4. **Inventory the mortgage** — who is on the note, current balance, current interest rate.
5. **Agree on a division method** — buyout, sale, deferred sale, or (rarely) ongoing co-ownership.
6. **Document in the marital settlement agreement** — who gets the property, who manages the transition, how rent is handled between signing and closing.
7. **Draft and record the quitclaim deed** — if one spouse retains, a Georgia real estate attorney prepares and records in the county where the property sits.
8. **Close the refinance** — the retaining spouse refinances to release the departing spouse from the original loan.
9. **Transfer the security deposit** — document in writing at time of transfer, per O.C.G.A. § 44-7-33.
10. **Notify the tenant in writing** — new owner's name and address for rent payment, promptly after transfer.
11. **Update insurance, accounts, and PM platform** — new landlord policy in the retaining spouse's name only; updated AppFolio or management records.
12. **Brief your CPA before year-end** — provide the divorce decree, the deed, and Form 4562 depreciation schedules.
