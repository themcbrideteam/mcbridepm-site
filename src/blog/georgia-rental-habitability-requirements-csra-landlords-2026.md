## What Georgia Law Actually Requires: The Habitability Baseline

Georgia landlord-tenant law is largely codified in Title 44, Chapter 7 of the Official Code of Georgia Annotated. The duty to maintain a rental in repair has always lived in O.C.G.A. § 44-7-13. What changed in 2024 is the scope and enforceability of that duty.

**Before the Safe at Home Act**, § 44-7-13 required landlords to keep premises in repair but did not expressly create a warranty of habitability. Courts recognized an implied warranty in certain circumstances, but it was inconsistently applied, and lease language could muddy the water.

**After the Safe at Home Act (HB 404, effective July 1, 2024)**, § 44-7-13(b) now includes an express, non-waivable warranty that rental premises will be "fit for human habitation." The [Georgia Appleseed Center for Law & Justice](https://gaappleseed.org/?p=3980), which advocated for the bill, summarizes the key change plainly: landlords can no longer use a lease clause to disclaim the duty to provide a habitable home.

Two other Safe at Home Act changes directly affect habitability compliance for CSRA landlords:

1. **Cooling is now a protected utility.** O.C.G.A. § 44-7-14.1 was amended to add cooling to the list of utilities a landlord cannot shut off — alongside heat, light, and water. In Augusta's climate, where summer temperatures routinely exceed 95°F, this is material. A working central air system is no longer just a market expectation; it is a legal one.

2. **Three-business-day cure before eviction.** A landlord pursuing a dispossessory for nonpayment must now provide three business days' notice before filing. This isn't a habitability rule directly, but it changes the eviction timeline that shapes how urgently habitability disputes need to be resolved before they escalate.

A second bill, HB 1171 (introduced in the 2026 legislative session), would establish a written list of specific minimum habitability standards in Georgia statute. As of early 2026, that bill had not received a floor vote. Until it passes — and it may not — "fit for human habitation" remains the operative standard, without a statutory item list. The practical result: landlords and courts use recognized industry standards to define the term.

## What "Fit for Human Habitation" Means in Practice

Because Georgia statute doesn't enumerate specific requirements, the most reliable proxy is HUD's Housing Quality Standards (HQS), the federal framework used to assess rentals for the Housing Choice Voucher (Section 8) program. The [HQS framework](https://www.hud.gov/program_offices/public_indian_housing/programs/hcv/hqs), used by housing authorities including Augusta Housing Authority, defines acceptable conditions across 13 categories. Any rental that passes HQS inspection is, by any reasonable reading, "fit for human habitation" under Georgia law.

Here is how the HQS categories translate to what your CSRA rental must have:

| HQS Category | What the landlord must provide |
|---|---|
| Sanitary facilities | Working toilet, tub or shower, hot and cold running water, all in usable condition |
| Food preparation | Working kitchen with sink and drain; space for a stove and refrigerator |
| Space and security | Every room must have an operable lock or window latch; exterior doors and windows must close and lock |
| Thermal environment | Heating system capable of maintaining 68°F in winter; cooling system capable of maintaining a healthy temperature in summer (post-Safe at Home Act) |
| Illumination and electricity | Adequate electrical supply; working outlets and fixtures in all rooms |
| Structure and materials | Roof, walls, floors, foundation, and stairs must be structurally sound with no severe damage |
| Interior air quality | No conditions producing dangerous levels of air pollution — carbon monoxide, mold, or chemical hazard |
| Water supply | Connection to safe potable water; no cross-connections with wastewater |
| Lead paint | Pre-1978 homes require lead paint disclosure; presence of deteriorating lead paint is a failure condition |
| Smoke detectors | Working smoke detector on every level and in or near every sleeping area |
| Site and neighborhood | Property must be accessible; access roads and walkways must be usable |
| Sanitary conditions | No evidence of pest infestation (roaches, rodents); trash removal available |
| Security | No conditions that create a clear danger to the health, life, or safety of occupants |

You don't have to accept Section 8 vouchers to benefit from this checklist — it is simply the clearest written definition of habitability available in the absence of a Georgia-specific statutory list.

## The Pre-Rental Habitability Checklist: 14 Items Before Move-In

Walk through every property — including ones that have been in your family for years and "seem fine" — using this checklist before listing. Issues you miss become your liability once a tenant moves in.

**Structural and exterior:**
- [ ] Roof — no missing shingles, no visible daylight from attic, gutters clear and draining away from foundation
- [ ] Foundation — no active water intrusion, no significant settling cracks in walls
- [ ] Windows — open, close, and lock; no broken panes, no rotted frames
- [ ] Exterior doors — open, close, and deadbolt properly; weatherstripping intact
- [ ] Steps, handrails, and walkways — stable, no tripping hazards

**Mechanical and plumbing:**
- [ ] HVAC — both heating and cooling functional; filter replaced; system services dated within 12 months
- [ ] Water heater — heating to 120°F (not higher than 130°F), no leaks, no rust on tank or supply lines
- [ ] All faucets — hot and cold present, no drips, reasonable water pressure
- [ ] All toilets — flush and refill completely, no running, no leaks at base
- [ ] Drains — no slow drains, no sewer odor in any room

**Electrical and safety:**
- [ ] All outlets — functional, no exposed wiring; GFCI protection in kitchen, bathrooms, garage, exterior
- [ ] Smoke detectors — one on each level, one in or near each sleeping area; batteries fresh
- [ ] CO detector — required if the home has gas appliances, attached garage, or gas furnace
- [ ] No active pest infestation — treat and document if any found before listing

If any item fails, repair it before the tenant moves in. Document every repair with dated photos. If you're converting a home you lived in yourself, pay particular attention to the HVAC system and smoke detectors — what passes for a comfortable owner-occupied home sometimes falls short of rental standards once the mechanical systems are assessed by a tenant or an inspector.

## What the Safe at Home Act Changed for Accidental Landlords

If you're a first-time landlord in Augusta or the surrounding CSRA, the Safe at Home Act's most practically important provision for you is the **non-waivability of the habitability warranty**. It means you cannot sidestep your obligations with a lease clause.

Many lease templates that circulate online — or that were drafted before July 2024 — still contain "as-is" language or provisions stating the tenant accepts the property in its current condition. Under O.C.G.A. § 44-7-13(b) as amended, those clauses are unenforceable. If you hand your tenant a lease with an "as-is" habitability waiver, the waiver doesn't protect you. You still owe a habitable property.

The [NLIHC summary of the Safe at Home Act](https://nlihc.org/resource/georgia-passes-new-tenant-protection-law-renters-establishing-minimum-habitability) describes it plainly: the law "establishes minimum habitability protections for renters" and does so in a way that cannot be contracted away.

Practically, this means:

- **Review your lease template** before every new tenancy. If it contains language disclaiming habitability, remove it or replace the template entirely.
- **Repair before you list.** The Act's warranty attaches when the lease is signed, but the standard expectation is that a habitable property is delivered at move-in.
- **Keep repair records.** If a tenant later claims the property wasn't habitable, your dated invoices, photos, and move-in inspection report are the evidence that you delivered a compliant property.

The Act also addressed the **cooling utility** issue that matters specifically in the CSRA. The Augusta area averages 36 days per year above 95°F. Under the amended § 44-7-14.1, a landlord cannot cut off the cooling system as a de facto eviction tactic. If the central air unit fails, you are responsible for repair — you cannot simply let a tenant swelter through an Augusta July.

## What Happens When Habitability Falls Short

Georgia law gives tenants specific remedies when a landlord fails to maintain habitable conditions. Understanding these helps you see what's at stake if you cut corners.

**Repair and deduct.** Under [O.C.G.A. § 44-7-14](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-44/chapter-7/article-1/section-44-7-14/), a tenant who gives written notice of a repair, allows a reasonable time for the landlord to act, and still faces a hazardous defect may have repairs made and deduct the cost from rent — up to one month's rent per repair event. This is the tenant's self-help remedy. It doesn't require the landlord's permission, but it does require prior written notice.

**Injunctive relief.** A tenant facing ongoing hazardous conditions can seek a court order requiring the landlord to make repairs. Courts in Richmond County Magistrate Court and Columbia County Magistrate Court have authority over these disputes.

**Lease termination.** If the condition is severe enough — total loss of essential services, structural danger — a tenant may be able to terminate the lease and claim constructive eviction. This is a fact-intensive analysis and typically requires a court ruling, but it's a real exposure for landlords who ignore serious defects.

**Criminal exposure.** Knowingly renting a property in uninhabitable condition to a tenant can, in extreme cases, reach into criminal housing code territory. The far more common outcome is civil liability and a disputed security deposit, but the floor of the exposure is worth knowing.

What Georgia law does **not** give tenants is the unilateral right to withhold rent. A tenant who stops paying rent in response to habitability complaints — without going through the repair-and-deduct process or a court order — is still in breach of the lease. An [iPropertyManagement analysis of Georgia landlord responsibilities](https://ipropertymanagement.com/laws/georgia-landlord-responsibilities) confirms that rent withholding is not a statutory remedy in Georgia. That said, a habitability defense can complicate an eviction proceeding and delay possession by weeks. The cleaner path is always to maintain the property.

## CSRA-Specific Considerations: Augusta, Columbia County, and Beyond

Georgia's habitability requirements apply statewide, but CSRA landlords face a few local factors worth knowing.

**No mandatory pre-rental registration (yet).** As of October 2026, neither Augusta-Richmond County nor Columbia County requires residential landlords to register their properties or pass a government inspection before renting. You don't need a landlord license in either jurisdiction. The habitability standard is enforced reactively — by complaint-driven code enforcement — rather than proactively.

However, **if your property participates in the Housing Choice Voucher program**, that changes. [Augusta Housing Authority](https://www.augustahousing.org/) requires a HUD Housing Quality Standards inspection before a voucher-assisted tenant can move in, and re-inspection every 24 months during the tenancy. Failing that inspection delays the lease start and delays your rental income. Our [guide to HCV landlords in the CSRA](/blog/housing-choice-voucher-landlord-guide-augusta-ga/) covers that process in detail.

**Fort Gordon's tenant pool.** A significant portion of CSRA tenants are active-duty service members stationed at Fort Gordon. Military households tend to be diligent about maintenance requests — they're used to well-maintained housing and report defects promptly. Providing a property that genuinely meets the habitability standard, not just technically clears it, is the best way to retain military tenants through their full assignment cycle.

**Pre-1978 homes.** If your inherited or converted home was built before 1978, federal law requires a lead paint disclosure to the tenant before the lease is signed. Properties with deteriorating lead-based paint require specific disclosures and potentially remediation. The [HUD lead paint disclosure rule (40 CFR Part 745)](https://www.hud.gov/program_offices/healthy_homes/lead/disclosure) applies regardless of Georgia state law.

For landlords in Evans (ZIP 30809 or 30813), Grovetown (ZIP 30813), Martinez (ZIP 30907), or North Augusta, SC, the checklist in this guide applies equally. South Carolina has its own warranty of habitability under the SC Residential Landlord and Tenant Act ([SC Code § 27-40-440](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t27c040.php)), but the practical standards are comparable.
