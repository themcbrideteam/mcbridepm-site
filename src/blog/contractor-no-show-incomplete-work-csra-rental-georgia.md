**What to do when a CSRA contractor no-shows:** Document the missed appointment in writing the same day. Notify your tenant with a revised timeline. Issue a written cure-or-cancel notice giving 24–72 hours to perform. If they don't respond, hire a replacement and pursue recovery through Georgia's licensing board complaint system (sos.ga.gov) and Magistrate Court (claims up to $15,000). Your habitability obligation to the tenant continues regardless of what the contractor does — the repair must still happen.

You hired someone to fix the HVAC at your rental in Evans. They took the deposit, confirmed the Thursday appointment — and then nothing. No show, no call, no text. Your tenant's been without heat for two days, and you're stuck holding a half-signed contract and a lump of dread.

This situation is more common than it should be, and accidental landlords — people who ended up with a rental because they moved, inherited a property, or couldn't sell — tend to get hit hardest. You don't yet have the vendor relationships that experienced investors build over years. You found someone on a referral app, paid a deposit, and trusted it would work.

Here's how to regain control, protect your tenant, and recover what you can.

## Understanding the Three Failure Modes — and Why It Matters

Not every contractor problem is the same situation legally, so naming what happened is the first step.

| Failure type | What it looks like | Your immediate exposure |
|---|---|---|
| **No-show** | Scheduled visit; contractor doesn't appear; no work started | Low immediate risk if repair isn't habitability-critical; escalates if you can't reach them |
| **Abandonment** | Work started, some payment made, contractor disappears mid-job | Higher risk — open work site, partial disruption, lien exposure |
| **Defective completion** | Contractor calls the job done; work fails inspection or breaks quickly | Liability depends on whether the defect affects habitability and whether you accepted payment |

Each starts with the same documentation step, but the escalation path and legal tools differ. This guide addresses all three, with emphasis on the most common scenario for smaller landlords: the contractor who took a deposit and never came back.

## Your 48-Hour Documentation Checklist

Speed matters. The window for strong documentation closes fast, and anything you do without a paper trail loses value later.

**Within the first 24 hours:**

- Send a timestamped text or email to the contractor stating the date, the agreed appointment time, and that they did not appear
- Photograph the unfinished work area — before anything is touched
- Pull up and screenshot your original text thread or email chain scheduling the visit
- Collect your payment records: bank statement, Venmo/Zelle screenshot, canceled check, or credit card transaction

**Within 48 hours:**

- Send a formal written notice (email plus certified mail) stating the contractor has 72 hours to contact you and schedule completion — or the contract is canceled and you'll engage a replacement at their expense
- Notify your tenant in writing with a revised completion date; if it's a habitability repair, give them a specific 48-hour window
- Get at least one quote from a replacement contractor so you have a comparison number

**Ongoing:**

- Save every incoming text, voicemail, and email — including silence (screenshots showing "no reply since [date]")
- Keep a simple running log: date, time, what happened, what you did

This packet is your evidence for a licensing board complaint, a demand letter, and a small claims filing.

## Georgia Habitability Law Doesn't Take a Day Off

Here's the part that surprises accidental landlords: the contractor's failure doesn't pause your legal obligation.

Under **O.C.G.A. §44-7-13**, Georgia landlords must keep rental property in a habitable condition — structurally sound, with functioning heating, cooling, and plumbing. Under **O.C.G.A. §44-7-14**, a landlord who fails to repair a known hazard is liable for injuries or damages that result.

The statute doesn't say "unless your contractor ghosted you." Your tenant is entitled to a habitable property. If the original contractor won't perform, your duty is to get a replacement, not to wait.

**What counts as a habitability repair** in Georgia:

- Heating and cooling failures (especially in extreme temperatures)
- Plumbing leaks or complete loss of running water
- Electrical hazards
- Active roof leaks that allow water intrusion
- Structural failures that make the property unsafe

For habitability repairs, the standard response window that courts and housing advocates treat as reasonable is **24–72 hours**. If a contractor fails to show for one of these repairs, you should be calling a replacement within 24 hours — not waiting to see if the original contractor surfaces.

**Georgia's Safe at Home Act** (HB 404, effective July 1, 2024, codified at O.C.G.A. §44-7-30.1 for newer leases) raised the floor on habitability enforcement and capped security deposits at two months' rent. Judges applying these standards pay attention to whether landlords acted with reasonable promptness when habitability issues were reported.

## Using Georgia Licensing Law as Leverage

Most landlords don't know this, but licensed contractors have something to lose beyond a contract dispute. Georgia requires licensing for contractors working on jobs over **$2,500** (O.C.G.A. §43-41-2), and the **State Licensing Board for Residential and General Contractors** has real teeth.

Under **O.C.G.A. §43-41-10**, the board can:

- Suspend or revoke a contractor's license
- Issue civil penalties
- Place conditions on future license renewals

For trade-specific work (plumbing, electrical, HVAC/mechanical), separate boards under the Secretary of State have the same authority.

**How to file:**

1. Go to **sos.ga.gov** → Licensing Boards → find the relevant board
2. Submit an online complaint with: contractor name and license number, your contract, payment documentation, communication records, and photos
3. The board assigns an investigator who contacts the contractor

This isn't a fast process — board investigations can take weeks — but it creates a formal record and puts the contractor's license on the line. Many contractors who would ignore a demand letter respond when a licensing board opens a file. It's also free to file.

**Check the license first.** Before hiring any contractor, verify their license at sos.ga.gov. If they're unlicensed and the job exceeds the threshold, they're in violation — and you have an additional avenue (and a stronger small claims argument) if something goes wrong.

## Mechanic's Lien Risk — the Problem You Didn't Expect

One thing abandonment cases create that no-shows typically don't: **mechanic's lien exposure**.

Under **O.C.G.A. §44-14-361**, a contractor has the right to file a lien on your property for the value of labor and materials furnished — even if the work is incomplete. A contractor who abandons mid-job can still file a lien for the work they did do, the materials they ordered, or both.

A lien on your rental property:

- Shows up in a title search
- Can complicate a future sale or refinance
- Must be formally contested if you believe it's inflated or unjustified

You can't ignore an abandonment and assume it goes away. Respond in writing. Document exactly what was and wasn't delivered. If the dollar value is significant (say, over $5,000), consult a Georgia real estate attorney before making additional payments or signing anything.

## Recovering Money in Georgia Magistrate Court

For most contractor disputes under $15,000, **Georgia Magistrate Court** (small claims) is the right venue. No attorney required. The filing fee is modest — typically $50–100 depending on the county. Cases in Richmond and Columbia County can often be heard within 30–60 days of filing.

**What you can claim:**

- Unearned deposits (money paid for work not performed)
- The cost difference between the abandoned contract price and what you paid a replacement to finish the job
- Documented out-of-pocket costs (materials you had to buy, temporary repairs, tenant accommodation if applicable)

**What you need to bring:**

- The original signed contract or written agreement
- Payment records showing what you paid and when
- Your complete communication log (texts, emails, voicemails)
- Photographs of the unfinished or defective work
- The replacement contractor's invoice
- Any licensing board complaint filing confirmation

The judge or magistrate will want to see that you gave the original contractor a reasonable chance to cure and that your replacement costs were reasonable. This is why the written cure notice step matters — it shows you weren't just unilaterally walking away from the contract.

If the original contractor has a **mechanic's lien** on record, the small claims pathway gets more complicated. That's when you need an attorney.

## Protecting an Open Work Site While You Wait

One of the worst outcomes in an abandonment scenario is a half-finished repair that creates a new hazard. Some specific situations to address before they become liability:

| Open work condition | Immediate risk | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Electrical panel open / wiring exposed | Fire hazard, shock risk | Do not leave open; call licensed electrician same day for interim make-safe |
| HVAC disconnected mid-install | No heat or cooling | Portable unit for tenant if gap will exceed 48 hours; document cost for recovery |
| Roof decking exposed / active leak risk | Water intrusion, mold risk | Temporary tarp immediately; get roofing contractor same day |
| Plumbing supply shut off and not restored | No running water | Critical habitability failure; get plumber same day regardless of original contract |

Document the open condition with photos before you touch anything. Then act — your tenant's habitability comes first.

## A Pre-Contract Checklist to Prevent This Next Time

After you've put this fire out, the most useful thing you can do is change how you hire. The contractors who ghost landlords are usually ones who were hired on price alone, with no vetting, no written scope, and payment in full upfront.

| Vetting step | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Verify license at sos.ga.gov before hiring | Confirms they're bonded and have a licensure record — something to lose |
| Get a written scope of work before any payment | Defines what "done" means — essential for a dispute |
| Use a payment milestone schedule | Deposit (25–30%), progress payment on rough-in, final payment on completion |
| Ask for two references on comparable jobs | A ghost rarely has verifiable past clients willing to talk |
| Get one competing quote | Keeps pricing honest and gives you a comparison if you need a replacement |
| Confirm insurance (GL + workers' comp) | Protects you from injury claims on your property |

This checklist takes about 30 minutes per contractor. It won't catch every bad actor, but it filters out the highest-risk category: unlicensed, uninsured workers who take upfront payment and disappear.
