## Why HOA Rental Restrictions Carry More Weight Than Zoning

Zoning tells you what can be built on a lot. It governs use categories — residential, commercial, mixed. For long-term residential rentals in Columbia County and Aiken County, local zoning is rarely the binding constraint. Property owners generally can rent their homes under residential zoning without a special permit or registration.

HOA-imposed restrictions are a different kind of limit entirely. They're private contractual obligations that run with the land — not government rules, but covenants you assume at closing. When you buy into an HOA community, you agree to its recorded Declaration and all subsequent validly-adopted amendments. That agreement binds you personally and survives a sale to the next buyer.

The practical consequence: an HOA can restrict when you can rent, to whom, for how long, and in what numbers — and enforce those restrictions by fining you, filing a lien against your property, or in extreme cases, filing for foreclosure if assessment fines go unpaid. This is general guidance from a property manager — not legal advice; a Georgia real estate attorney can advise on your specific situation.

What zoning cannot do, an HOA often can. The research process begins with understanding exactly what your target subdivision's governing documents say.

## The Two Types of Georgia HOA Community — and Why It Changes Everything

Not all Georgia homeowners associations have the same legal powers, and the distinction matters significantly for investors.

**POAA communities** have elected to be governed by the Georgia Property Owners' Association Act, codified at O.C.G.A. §§ 44-3-220 through 44-3-235. This opt-in election is made in the recorded Declaration. These communities get enhanced legal powers — including the ability to file assessment liens and pursue non-judicial foreclosure — but they also get more constraints on what amendments they can adopt.

**Common-law communities** have not made this POAA election. They operate under general contract principles and O.C.G.A. § 44-5-60 (covering the creation and enforcement of covenants). Their powers are narrower, but so are some of the protections they provide to owners.

To determine which type your target community is, search the Declaration for any of these phrases:
- "affirmative election"
- "Georgia Property Owners' Association Act"
- "Article 6 of Chapter 3 of Title 44"
- "O.C.G.A. §§ 44-3-220 et seq."

If you find this language, you're in a POAA community. If you don't, the community is likely a common-law HOA — though an older declaration may reference predecessor statutes, so a Georgia real estate attorney familiar with community association law should confirm if you're unsure.

This distinction matters for one specific and very important reason.

## Georgia SB 442 (2020): The Rental Protection That Changed Everything for POAA Investors

On August 5, 2020, Governor Kemp signed [SB 442](https://gov.georgia.gov/document/2020-signed-legislation/sb-442/download), which revised O.C.G.A. § 44-3-226(a) — the POAA's section governing the amendment of governing instruments. The law prohibits amendments to POAA instruments that restrict the rental of residential lots. It became effective January 1, 2021.

What this means in plain terms: if your target community is POAA-governed and had no rental restriction in its declaration as of January 1, 2021, the HOA cannot adopt one now. Any amendment purporting to add a new rental cap, minimum lease-term requirement, or owner-occupancy rule to a POAA community after that date is prohibited under state law.

This is a meaningful protection for investors buying in established Columbia County subdivisions. Many of the larger, well-maintained communities in Evans and Grovetown that pre-date the explosion of HOA formation in the 2000s and 2010s elected POAA status and had no rental restriction in their original declaration. If the management company tells you "we're thinking about adding a rental cap," you can point to § 44-3-226(a) and SB 442.

**Critical limits of SB 442:**
- It is not retroactive. It does not strike rental restrictions that were validly adopted before January 1, 2021. If a POAA community imposed a cap in 2018 through a two-thirds member vote — as upheld by the Georgia Court of Appeals in [*Pasha v. Battle Creek Homeowners Ass'n*](https://cases.justia.com/georgia/court-of-appeals/2019-a19a0015.pdf) — that cap remains valid and enforceable.
- It applies only to POAA communities. Common-law HOAs are governed by different rules; in those communities, the amendment process for adding restrictions depends on the declaration's amendment clause and O.C.G.A. § 44-5-60.
- Short-term rental restrictions are a gray area. The 2020 amendment addresses "rental of residential lots" broadly, but whether STR-specific rules (Airbnb, stays under 30 days) are covered is not fully settled. For short-term rental purposes, treat this as an open legal question until there is clearer case law or statutory guidance.

For long-term rental investors, the practical rule is: in a POAA community with no pre-2021 rental cap, you have strong protection against one being added. In a POAA community that had a cap before 2021 or in any common-law community, the existing rules apply and you need to know what they say.


## How to Pull the Recorded Documents from Columbia County Superior Court

The Columbia County Clerk of Superior Court maintains the county's official real estate records, including every recorded Declaration of Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions. These documents are public records. You don't need an attorney or a title company to access them — you need the recorded subdivision name and a few minutes on the online portal.

**Step 1: Get the recorded subdivision name.** The marketing name ("Stonecreek," "Summer Lakes," "Evans Station") is not always identical to the recorded name. Ask the listing agent for the recorded subdivision name as it appears on the most recent deed. Alternatively, pull the property's tax record from the Columbia County Tax Commissioner website and look for the subdivision or plat reference.

**Step 2: Search the Clerk of Courts portal.** The Columbia County Clerk of Superior Court's website links to their real property records search. Search by grantor/grantee name using the homeowners association's name, or by the subdivision name in the instrument description field. You're looking for a "Declaration of Covenants" or "Declaration and Protective Covenants" — the original recording, plus any recorded amendments.

**Step 3: Download and read all amendments.** The original declaration may have been recorded in the 1990s or early 2000s. Rental restrictions are frequently added or modified through amendments. Download every amendment and read them in chronological order. An amendment adding a rental cap ten years after the original recording is just as enforceable as language in the original document — provided it was validly adopted.

**Step 4: Look for the POAA election.** In the original declaration or in an amendment adopting POAA status, look for the phrases identified in the previous section. POAA elections are sometimes added years after the community formed.

**Step 5: Note the amendment threshold.** Under O.C.G.A. § 44-3-226, POAA declarations can be amended by owners holding two-thirds of the voting interest, unless the declaration requires a higher threshold (up to 80%). If you're evaluating a future risk — could this community add a restriction? — the amendment threshold tells you how difficult that would be.

If the online records portal is unclear or the documents are hard to read (some older declarations were scanned from paper copies), the Columbia County Clerk of Courts office in Evans can assist. Title companies doing your closing search will also pull these records, but by then you've already written the earnest-money check.

## What to Look for in a CC&R: The Six Rental Provisions That Affect Investors

Once you have the declaration and amendments in hand, search for each of these:

| Provision | What to look for | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| Rental cap | Percentage limit (e.g., "not more than 15% of homes shall be rented") | May place you on a waitlist if at capacity; ask management company for current count |
| Minimum lease term | "No lease shall be for a term of less than 6 months" or "12 months" | Bars month-to-month tenants; check if this conflicts with your BAH tenant strategy |
| Tenant approval | "Owner shall submit tenant information to the Association for approval prior to occupancy" | Adds 5–15 business days to your tenant placement timeline; some have an application fee |
| Owner-occupancy requirement | "Owners must have occupied the property as a primary residence for at least 12 months before renting" | Could bar you from renting immediately after closing if you never occupied it |
| Short-term rental ban | "No rentals for a term of less than 30 days" or explicit Airbnb / vacation rental language | Prevents Masters week rentals, Airbnb, VRBO regardless of Augusta's separate zoning rules |
| Subletting prohibition | "Tenant may not sublet" or "no room rentals" | Affects tenant's ability to bring in roommates and your screening process |

If none of these appear in the recorded Declaration or any amendment, that's a good sign — but confirm with the management company that there are no unrecorded rules purporting to restrict rentals (which, under Georgia law, are generally not enforceable anyway).

One note on short-term rentals: Augusta-Richmond County is currently undergoing its [first major zoning overhaul since 1963](https://www.wrdw.com/2026/10/20/augusta-considers-first-zoning-overhaul-since-1963/), and earlier drafts included provisions that would have restricted Masters week rentals. That provision was removed from the current draft, but the situation is evolving. For long-term residential rentals in Columbia County, municipal zoning is rarely the operative constraint — HOA covenants are. For short-term rental investors specifically, follow both tracks.

## Assessing Whether a Rental Cap Is at Capacity

Finding a rental cap in the CC&Rs is step one. Finding out whether that cap is currently at capacity is step two — and they're separate research tasks.

Call or email the HOA management company directly. Ask:
- What is the current number of rented homes in the community?
- What is the cap?
- Is there a waitlist? If so, what is its current length?
- How long have recent waitlist periods been?

Some management companies will give you this information freely before you're under contract. Others will ask that you submit a request or wait until you have a signed purchase agreement. Either way, get the answer in writing — a verbal confirmation that "we're at 10 out of 30" is worth less than a written email from the management company.

If the community is at capacity, calculate the waitlist cost with real numbers. If a three-bedroom Evans home rents for $2,100 per month and the waitlist is six months, you're carrying $12,600 in deferred revenue, plus whatever you're spending on mortgage, insurance, taxes, and utilities during the vacancy. That changes the investment math meaningfully and should factor into your offer price.

Grandfathering — the idea that if you're already renting before a cap is imposed, your lease isn't cut short — applies in a specific way. Under O.C.G.A. § 44-3-226(a), in POAA communities, an owner who was legally renting before a new restriction was adopted has certain continuing rights. But as noted above, in a POAA community governed by SB 442, no new restriction can be added at all after January 1, 2021. This means grandfathering is primarily relevant in communities where a cap already existed and you're trying to understand how a future lease would be treated. For a detailed walkthrough of the grandfathering rules specifically for PCS landlords, see [HOA Rental Rules in Evans and Grovetown: A PCS Landlord Guide](/blog/hoa-rental-rules-evans-grovetown-pcs-landlords/).


## What Georgia SB 406 Adds Starting January 1, 2027

Governor Kemp signed [SB 406](https://www.wrdw.com/2026/05/14/new-ga-hoa-law-raises-foreclosure-threshold-adds-new-protections/), the Georgia Property Owners' Bill of Rights Act, on May 12, 2026. Its most significant provisions for landlords in HOA communities take effect January 1, 2027 — five weeks from now.

**HOA registration requirement.** Starting January 1, 2027, every Georgia HOA must register annually with the [Georgia Secretary of State's Property Owners' Associations Division](https://sos.ga.gov/page/georgia-property-owners-associations-division-faq). The annual fee is $100. The Secretary of State's office is currently finalizing the registry implementation; the full scope of what the public registry will display is still being clarified through the rulemaking process.

**Enforcement consequences for non-registration.** An HOA that fails to register loses its authority to collect fines, file assessment liens, or pursue foreclosure under the POAA. For landlords, this creates a practical tool: if your HOA disputes a tenant's lease, you can check registration status and challenge any enforcement action if the HOA hasn't registered.

**Dispute resolution process.** SB 406 creates a formal administrative complaint process for disputes between owners and HOAs. Rather than going directly to civil court, owners can file a complaint with the Secretary of State's office. This lowers the cost of challenging an HOA that acts outside its authority — including an HOA that attempts to enforce an invalid rental restriction.

**Foreclosure threshold change.** The act raises the amount of unpaid assessments required before an HOA can initiate foreclosure. Under the new rule, the threshold is the lesser of $4,000 or 12 months of assessments, and must not be less than $2,000. For landlords who have ever faced aggressive HOA fine enforcement, this is a meaningful protection.

**Attorney's fees provision (already in effect).** The attorney's fees section of SB 406 took effect July 1, 2026 and applies to all actions filed on or after that date. This provision allows courts to award attorney's fees in HOA disputes, which makes it more viable for individual owners to challenge HOA overreach.

## Your HOA Due Diligence Checklist Before Closing

Use this as a template when evaluating any HOA-governed property in Columbia County, Richmond County, or Aiken County.

**Before you submit an offer:**
- [ ] Get the full legal name of the homeowners association from the listing agent or tax record
- [ ] Identify the recorded subdivision plat name (may differ from marketing name)
- [ ] Pull the Declaration and all recorded amendments from Columbia County Clerk of Superior Court
- [ ] Confirm whether the community is POAA-governed (look for POAA election language)
- [ ] Identify any rental cap, minimum lease term, tenant approval requirement, or owner-occupancy rule
- [ ] Note the amendment threshold and whether a future restriction addition is legally possible

**While under contract:**
- [ ] Contact the HOA management company in writing: ask for current rental count, cap status, and waitlist length
- [ ] Request a copy of the community's current House Rules or Addendum to Covenants (may contain leasing rules not in the declaration — confirm whether these are recorded or unrecorded)
- [ ] Confirm the monthly or annual HOA assessment and any special assessments pending
- [ ] Verify any tenant approval requirements and typical turnaround time
- [ ] Confirm the community's short-term rental position (Airbnb / VRBO / Masters week)

**Before closing:**
- [ ] Review title commitment for any recorded covenants or restrictions not in the declaration
- [ ] Confirm whether the HOA is current on SB 406 registration (starting January 2027) or has a plan to register
- [ ] Confirm whether any amendment to the declaration was adopted after January 1, 2021 purporting to restrict rentals (in a POAA community, this amendment is prohibited under SB 442)
- [ ] Consult a Georgia real estate attorney if any provision is unclear or potentially unenforceable

**At closing:**
- [ ] Obtain the contact name and direct email of the HOA management company's property management liaison
- [ ] Confirm in writing how tenant approval notices should be submitted and to whom
- [ ] Add HOA management company to your post-closing contacts alongside McBride PM's team
