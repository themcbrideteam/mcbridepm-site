You bought a home in Evans for $295,000 eighteen months ago and listed it with McBride Property Management. The property is generating solid cash flow, your CPA did your 2025 return, and somewhere in the Schedule E work you noticed that your annual depreciation deduction—around $7,600 on roughly $247,000 in building basis—felt underwhelming compared to what you've read about real estate tax strategy.

It is. Standard straight-line residential depreciation divides the building's cost evenly across 27.5 years. That is IRS-mandated math, not an optimization. Cost segregation is the optimization—and for investors buying or already owning single-family rentals in Columbia County, Richmond County, or Aiken County, 2026 is the right moment to understand it.

## What Cost Segregation Actually Does

Standard depreciation treats a residential rental as a single asset with a 27.5-year life under MACRS (Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System). Cost segregation disaggregates the building into its component parts and assigns each one the MACRS life that the [IRS Rev. Proc. 87-56](https://www.irs.gov/irb/1987-23_IRB#RP-87-56) asset class tables allow.

The result: a portion of what you bought gets moved from the 27.5-year column into the 5-year or 15-year column. With 100% bonus depreciation currently in effect for qualifying property, that reclassified portion is fully deducted in the year it is placed in service—not over the remaining depreciation schedule.

The technique is not a gray area. Courts have affirmed it. The IRS addresses it directly in [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946), which covers how to depreciate property. Engineering firms certified by the [American Society of Cost Segregation Professionals (ASCSP)](https://www.ascsp.org/) provide the documentation that supports the reclassification and defends it under IRS examination.

This is general guidance from a property manager—not legal or tax advice. Talk to a Georgia or South Carolina CPA and, for significant properties, a qualified cost segregation engineer before making decisions based on this post.

## The Four Asset Classes in a CSRA Single-Family Rental

A cost segregation study on an Evans, Grovetown, or Martinez home sorts every component into one of four buckets. The study engineer walks the property (or reviews photos, floor plans, and construction records for a desktop study) and assigns each element its proper MACRS life.

| Asset Class | MACRS Life | Common Components | Typical CSRA SFR Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal property | 5 years | Flooring, appliances, cabinets, decorative lighting, window treatments | Carpet, vinyl plank, refrigerator, range, dishwasher, light fixtures |
| Personal property | 7 years | Some specialized equipment, office fixtures | Less common in a pure SFR |
| Land improvements | 15 years | Landscaping, driveways, flatwork, fencing, retaining walls, outdoor lighting, irrigation | Concrete driveway, sidewalks, patio, privacy fence, shrubs and plantings |
| Residential real property | 27.5 years | Structural elements, roof, HVAC, plumbing, walls, windows, foundation | The building structure itself |

For a typical $280,000 Evans home where land accounts for approximately 18% of value, the depreciable building basis is roughly $229,600. Studies suggest 15–30% of that depreciable basis—call it $34,000 to $69,000—may be reclassifiable to 5- or 15-year property. The remainder stays at 27.5 years and continues to depreciate on the standard schedule.


## The 100% Bonus Depreciation Restoration: What Changed in 2025

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) initially set bonus depreciation at 100% for qualifying property placed in service between 2017 and 2022, then scheduled it to phase down annually: 80% in 2023, 60% in 2024, 40% in 2025, 20% in 2026, and zero in 2027. For investors who bought in 2025 expecting the 40% rate, then discovered the 2026 rate had dropped to 20%, the phase-down was a source of frustration.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (P.L. 119-21), signed July 4, 2025, reversed course. It restored 100% bonus depreciation for qualified property acquired after January 19, 2025, and permanently removed the sunset. The 20% 2026 rate under TCJA no longer applies to post-January 2025 acquisitions.

For a CSRA investor, the math shifts substantially:

**Without cost segregation:** A $229,600 depreciable basis on a $280,000 Evans home yields approximately $8,349 per year in straight-line depreciation.

**With cost segregation and 100% bonus depreciation on a $50,000 reclassification (illustrative):** You deduct $50,000 in year one on the reclassified components, plus $6,527 on the remaining $179,600 in year one at 27.5 years. First-year total depreciation: approximately $56,527 versus $8,349 without the study.

The incremental first-year deduction in this example is roughly $48,000. At a 32% federal marginal rate, that is approximately $15,360 in tax deferred in year one alone. A study costing $4,000 pays for itself more than three times over in that single year—provided you have the passive income or status to use the deduction.

Important precision: "deferred" and "eliminated" are not the same. All prior depreciation is recaptured when you sell. The benefit is the time value of money.

## Does Cost Segregation Make Sense for a CSRA SFR? A Decision Checklist

Not every property or owner profile is a good cost segregation candidate. Work through this before commissioning a study:

**Strong candidate — cost segregation likely pencils out:**
- [ ] Property acquisition price is $150,000 or higher (study cost is disproportionate for lower-priced properties)
- [ ] Property was acquired after January 19, 2025 (100% bonus depreciation applies)
- [ ] You have passive income from other rentals that the additional deductions can offset, OR you have real estate professional status (750+ material participation hours annually in real estate)
- [ ] You are in the 22% federal bracket or higher
- [ ] You are not planning to sell within 2–3 years (recapture math becomes unfavorable on a very short hold)

**Model carefully before proceeding:**
- [ ] You own only one rental, have no passive income, and are not a real estate professional (losses carry forward but create no immediate tax benefit)
- [ ] The property was acquired before January 19, 2025 (the look-back study still works, but the 100% bonus rate may not apply to the reclassified components—confirm with your CPA)
- [ ] You have significant suspended passive losses from prior years already

**Cost segregation probably doesn't help here:**
- [ ] You are in the 10–12% federal bracket (the deduction benefit is too small relative to study cost)
- [ ] You are planning a sale or exchange within 12 months

## Catch-Up Depreciation: If You Already Own a CSRA Rental, You Haven't Missed Out

A common misconception: cost segregation only benefits properties you just purchased. The IRS permits a look-back study on properties you've owned for years.

The mechanic is [IRS Form 3115, Change in Accounting Method](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-3115). By filing Form 3115 with your return, you claim the cumulative "excess" depreciation—the difference between what cost segregation would have yielded since you placed the property in service and what you actually claimed—as a lump-sum deduction in the current year. This is an automatic method change. The IRS does not require prior approval and you do not amend prior returns.

**Example:** You bought a Grovetown home in 2022 and have been depreciating it straight-line. If a cost segregation study determines $45,000 should have been classified as 5- and 15-year property, and you've held the property for four years, you've missed a meaningful deduction amount. A Form 3115 lets you claim that entire catch-up adjustment on your 2026 return.

The catch-up does not qualify for 100% bonus depreciation (since the property was placed in service before the OBBBA cutoff date). It is claimed as the computed catch-up deduction. Still valuable, especially at a 32% marginal rate and when you have passive income to absorb it.


## Passive Activity Rules: The Variable That Determines How Much You Actually Save

Depreciation deductions from rental property are passive losses under the IRS passive activity rules. Passive losses can only offset passive income unless you qualify under two exceptions:

**The $25,000 offset allowance:** If you actively participate in managing your rental and your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) is below $100,000, you can deduct up to $25,000 of passive rental losses against ordinary income. This allowance phases out between $100,000 and $150,000 MAGI and disappears entirely above $150,000.

**Real estate professional status:** If you spend more than 750 hours per year in qualifying real estate activities—and that total exceeds your time in any other profession—your rental losses are treated as non-passive and can offset ordinary income without limit.

For many out-of-state investors who own CSRA rentals as part of a diversified portfolio—professionals with MAGI above $150,000 who don't work in real estate as their primary occupation—neither exception applies. In that case, cost segregation losses carry forward on Schedule E and are released in full when the property is sold. The benefit is real but deferred: you get a large deduction in a future year rather than immediately.

The investors who get the most immediate value are those who (a) own multiple CSRA rentals generating positive passive income, with accelerated depreciation from new acquisitions offsetting that income, or (b) qualify as real estate professionals.

Understanding where you fall on the passive activity spectrum is the most important question to answer before commissioning a study. This is a conversation for your CPA, not something to guess at.

## How to Commission a Cost Segregation Study

The process is straightforward for a CSRA single-family rental:

1. **Find a qualified provider.** The [ASCSP](https://www.ascsp.org/) maintains a provider directory of credentialed engineers. Most firms work remotely—you do not need a provider physically located in Augusta, though familiarity with southeastern construction standards (brick ranch, board-and-batten, Craftsman construction typical in Columbia County) can improve the accuracy of minor component allocations.

2. **Provide property information.** The engineer needs your purchase settlement statement or closing disclosure, floor plans or square footage measurements, an itemized list of improvements made since purchase, and your acquisition date.

3. **Receive the study report.** A complete report separates every building component by asset class, provides the allocated basis for each, and documents the methodology in a form that defends the reclassification in an examination.

4. **Give the report to your CPA.** They handle the Schedule E entries, Form 4562 (Depreciation and Amortization), and—if applicable—Form 3115 for a look-back study.

For a 2026 tax year benefit, the study should be delivered before December 31, 2026. Commission it in October or November. A December 29 request to an engineering firm is a request for the study to miss the tax year.

## Depreciation Recapture: The Back End You Must Model

Accelerated depreciation is not a tax forgiveness—it is a tax deferral. When you sell a property, all prior depreciation is recaptured:

- **Section 1250 recapture** (the 27.5-year building components) is taxed at a maximum federal rate of 25% as unrecaptured Section 1250 gain.
- **Section 1245 recapture** (personal property—the 5-year items accelerated under cost segregation) is taxed at ordinary income tax rates, which are typically higher than the 25% Section 1250 rate.

For investors who front-load depreciation heavily through cost segregation, the Section 1245 recapture on personal property generates a tax bill at ordinary rates when the property is eventually sold. This is the core tradeoff: you got the deductions early, at the cost of recapturing them later at ordinary rather than capital gains rates.

The standard mitigation is a [1031 exchange](/blog/1031-exchange-basics-augusta-rental-investors/). A properly executed exchange defers all recapture into the replacement property. If you hold CSRA rentals long-term and exchange into replacement property, the recapture is perpetually deferred—and potentially eliminated entirely at death through estate step-up in basis. The [depreciation recapture post](/blog/depreciation-recapture-selling-rental-property-georgia/) on this site covers the mechanics of what happens when you sell a Georgia rental after taking significant depreciation.

Plan this side of the equation before you commission the study, not after.

## Integrating Cost Segregation With Your CSRA Portfolio Strategy

For McBride PM clients who own multiple units in Evans, Grovetown, Martinez, or other CSRA submarkets, cost segregation can function as a tax lever across the portfolio rather than a one-time study:

**New acquisitions (post-January 19, 2025):** Commission a study in year one. Capture 100% bonus depreciation on reclassified components. Use the resulting passive losses to offset passive income from other properties in the portfolio.

**Existing properties acquired before the OBBBA cutoff:** File Form 3115 for a look-back catch-up deduction. No amendment, current-year benefit based on accumulated missed depreciation.

**Properties nearing a sale:** Decide whether cost segregation makes sense given the recapture exposure. In most cases on a short remaining hold, it doesn't—but model it. If you are holding a property that has appreciated significantly and plan to exchange into a larger asset, additional depreciation recapture upon sale is deferred anyway.

The [Operating Expenses Worksheet](/pdfs/McBride-PM_Operating-Expenses-Worksheet.pdf) McBride PM provides to owners includes depreciation as a line item in the net operating income calculation. If your worksheet reflects only straight-line 27.5-year amortization, your true NOI picture understates the tax shelter your property provides. Revisit that calculation with a cost segregation study in hand.
