Every week, a property owner in the Augusta area calls us with a version of the same problem: a tenant moved out, the water bill is past due in the landlord's name, or a service was transferred without notice, or a lease says "utilities included" without explaining what that actually means. These calls share a common root — utility responsibility was treated as an afterthought.

Utilities are not an afterthought. In Georgia, they sit at the intersection of habitability law, lease contract, and tenant relations. Get them wrong and you're exposed to legal liability, unexpected costs, and disputes that complicate the already complex work of owning rental property. Get them right and they become a non-issue — something your lease handles quietly, every lease term.

This guide walks through Georgia's legal framework, the common utility arrangements you'll encounter across the CSRA, the lease language that protects owners, and the mistakes that turn utility questions into expensive problems.

---

## The Legal Foundation: Georgia's Habitability Statute

Georgia's residential landlord-tenant law is relatively landlord-friendly compared to many other states, but it draws a clear line around habitability. Under **O.C.G.A. § 44-7-13**, landlords are required to:

- Keep the premises in repair
- Maintain electrical and plumbing systems in good working order
- Keep the property in a habitable, safe condition

This habitability floor matters for utility purposes in a specific way: a landlord must ensure that the *infrastructure* for essential utilities exists and functions. If a water heater fails, the landlord must repair it. If the electrical panel needs upgrading to safely serve modern appliances, that's the landlord's responsibility. The obligation is to the *system*, not the ongoing *bill*.

Nothing in O.C.G.A. Title 44, Chapter 7 requires a Georgia landlord to pay the monthly charges for electricity, gas, water, sewer, or trash collection. The allocation of those ongoing costs is left entirely to the lease agreement.

**The critical companion statute is O.C.G.A. § 44-7-14.1**, which prohibits self-help eviction — including utility cutoff. If a tenant is behind on rent and a landlord calls the power company to terminate service hoping the tenant will leave, that is an illegal act. The landlord faces civil liability for actual damages and potentially punitive damages. The dispossessory process, slow as it feels, is the only legal path.

This matters even when utilities are in the landlord's name. If a tenant's lease gives them the right to occupy the property, the landlord cannot unilaterally deprive them of habitability-related services. The rule applies regardless of who holds the utility account.

## What the Lease Decides: The Spectrum of Utility Arrangements

Within the law's constraints, lease terms control everything. CSRA rentals use several common utility structures, each with distinct tradeoffs:

### Tenant-Responsible Utilities (Most Common in Single-Family)

For the majority of single-family homes in Augusta, Evans, Grovetown, and Martinez, the lease requires the tenant to establish their own accounts for all utilities before or at move-in. The landlord pays nothing beyond any services contractually included.

This arrangement is clean, simple, and limits landlord exposure. When a tenant doesn't pay the power bill, it affects the tenant's service — not the landlord's property account. When a tenant vacates, they cancel their own accounts.

**The key requirement:** your lease must state clearly and specifically which utilities the tenant is responsible for. "Tenant pays utilities" is vague and has been disputed in Georgia courts. Name the utility providers and the accounts: Georgia Power, Augusta Utilities for water and sewer, Atlanta Gas Light or Columbia Gas for gas, Augusta Waste Management for trash. Ambiguity is your liability.

### Landlord-Included Utilities (Less Common, Usually for Multi-Unit or Furnished Properties)

Some landlords include one or more utilities in rent — typically water and sewer for properties where individual sub-meters don't exist, or in furnished properties aimed at corporate or military housing. When a landlord includes utilities:

- The rental rate must account for the full expected utility cost, plus a buffer for unusual usage
- The lease should cap tenant usage or define reasonable use to prevent abuse
- The landlord carries the risk of rate increases and conservation failures

Including utilities makes marketing simpler ("all-inclusive rent of $1,850") but transfers consumption risk to the owner. It works well for water and trash, where tenant influence on costs is limited. It works poorly for electricity and gas, where tenant behavior — leaving windows open in July with the AC running — can double expected costs.

### Split Arrangements

Some properties use a hybrid: landlord covers water and trash (where individual metering is unavailable or impractical), tenant covers electricity and gas. This is common in smaller multi-unit properties and older homes where water meters were never separated.

## Setting Up Utilities at Move-In: Protecting Your Investment

The move-in day utility transfer is where most problems start. A tenant who takes possession before utilities are in their name, or who is given keys before account numbers are confirmed, creates exposure for the landlord. Here's the process that eliminates that risk:

**Before the lease is signed:**
Confirm which utilities serve the property and get the account numbers. For Augusta addresses, this means Augusta Utilities for water and sewer (even properties served by Georgia Power for electric), Georgia Power for electricity, and Atlanta Gas Light for gas on homes with gas appliances. For Evans and Martinez properties in Columbia County, water is typically Columbia County Utilities.

**At lease signing:**
Require the tenant to show proof of utility account applications or transfers before the keys change hands. Many property managers require this as a move-in condition, with a 24-hour window to submit confirmation numbers. No confirmation, no keys.

**At move-in inspection:**
Document meter readings for all utilities in the move-in report. This creates an irrefutable record of starting usage and protects you in the event of a disputed final bill at move-out.

**Keep the account numbers:**
Maintain a file with each utility provider's account number, service address, and the date of tenant transfer. If a tenant fails to pay and the utility company tries to rebill the owner, you need that documentation.

One practical protection: establish a small landlord-held account with Augusta Utilities for water and sewer service on your properties. This "safety net" account can restore service immediately if a tenant's account lapses, preventing a freeze burst or mold-generating humidity problem. You then recover the cost through the security deposit or lease enforcement.

## The Utility Cutoff Trap

The most expensive utility mistake Georgia landlords make has nothing to do with who pays the monthly bill. It's leaving utilities in the landlord's name after a tenant occupies the property.

It happens most often in properties that were previously owner-occupied — the landlord lists the house quickly, signs a lease, hands over the keys, and forgets to transfer the Georgia Power account. The tenant lives there for eight months without ever paying the electric bill. When the tenant moves out owing three months' back rent, the Georgia Power bill is also delinquent — and it's in the owner's name.

**The fix is procedural, not legal:** utility transfer is a move-in checklist item, not an afterthought. Treat it with the same non-negotiable urgency as a signed lease and a collected security deposit.

If a tenant genuinely cannot establish utility service (poor credit with the utility provider, prior delinquency), that is relevant information about the tenancy. Some utilities require deposits for residents with poor payment histories; a tenant who cannot post a utility deposit is showing you something about their financial stability.

## Aiken and North Augusta: South Carolina Properties

Landlords who own rental property in Aiken County or North Augusta operate under South Carolina's Residential Landlord and Tenant Act (SC Code Title 27, Chapter 40) rather than Georgia's O.C.G.A. Title 44. The frameworks are similar, but owners with properties in both states should understand the key distinctions:

**Habitability standard:** SC Code § 27-40-440 imposes the same landlord obligation to maintain functional systems. The legal prohibition on self-help utility termination is equally clear under South Carolina law.

**Utility providers:** Aiken County electric service is primarily Duke Energy (formerly SCE&G in some areas). Water and sewer are often the City of Aiken Utilities, though rural Aiken County properties may use private well and septic. North Augusta properties near the Georgia line may have Augusta Utilities service despite the SC address — confirm this early, as it surprises owners.

**Security deposits:** South Carolina permits landlords to hold up to two months' rent as a security deposit (SC Code § 27-40-410), compared to Georgia's absence of a statutory cap. This slightly larger deposit gives SC landlords more coverage for utility disputes at move-out.

**Lease requirements:** As in Georgia, the SC lease should name specific utility providers and account responsibilities. "Tenant pays utilities" is insufficient in South Carolina courts as well.

## When Landlords Pay Utilities: Making It Work

If your property or market requires landlord-paid utilities, here's how to structure it to protect your margin:

**Cap usage in the lease.** A utility addendum can establish a baseline monthly allocation — say, $150/month for electricity — and require the tenant to pay any overage. This is legal in Georgia and shifts consumption risk back to the tenant.

**Choose which utilities to include carefully.** Water and trash are the most predictable; include these before electricity or gas. A tenant who runs the dishwasher twice a day costs you little more than one who doesn't. A tenant who runs the heat to 80°F in January with the windows cracked costs you significantly more.

**Build it into your underwriting.** If you've included water and trash in a $1,850/month rent in Evans, that's effectively a $1,700/month rent with a $150/month utility cushion. Model this explicitly in your cash flow analysis — the cushion should cover 110-120% of your average utility cost to account for seasonal variation and tenant turnover months.

**Sub-metering for multi-unit properties.** If you own a duplex or small apartment building in Augusta with a single water meter, consider installing sub-meters. Georgia permits sub-metering; the cost of the equipment (typically $200–$500 per unit for water) is often recovered within two years in reduced utility expenses. You cannot charge tenants more than the utility's retail rate per gallon, but you recover the full cost of water use rather than absorbing it.

## Checklist: Utility Responsibility at Every Stage

**Before listing:**
- [ ] Confirm all utility providers serving the property (electric, gas, water/sewer, trash)
- [ ] Document account numbers and current service status
- [ ] Decide which utilities will be tenant-held vs. landlord-included
- [ ] Include utility responsibility explicitly in listing description

**Lease signing:**
- [ ] Name each utility provider and responsibility in the lease
- [ ] Include utility transfer as a move-in condition (keys released after confirmation)
- [ ] If including utilities, add a usage cap addendum
- [ ] Confirm tenant's ability to establish utility accounts (flag deposit requirements)

**Move-in day:**
- [ ] Collect utility account confirmation numbers or transfer documentation
- [ ] Record meter readings for all utilities in the move-in report
- [ ] Confirm all utilities are in tenant's name before releasing keys

**During tenancy:**
- [ ] Conduct periodic utility checks (at annual renewals) to confirm accounts are current
- [ ] Document any utility-related maintenance (water heater repair, HVAC service) with dates

**Move-out:**
- [ ] Conduct utility status check at lease end
- [ ] Establish temporary landlord accounts for vacancy period
- [ ] Document final meter readings
- [ ] Apply security deposit toward any landlord-incurred utility costs caused by tenant
