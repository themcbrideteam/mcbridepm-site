## Why the CSRA Contractor Market Is Harder Than It Looks

Finding skilled contractors in Augusta is a known challenge among rental investors. On real-estate investing forums like BiggerPockets, threads about Augusta contractors generate more frustrated responses than almost any other topic. Forum regulars warn that the CSRA has no shortage of people who call themselves contractors, but that the gap between someone who answers their phone and someone who does quality, licensed, insured work is wide.

Part of the problem is structural. Columbia County has seen rapid residential growth—Grovetown, Evans, and Harlem have all expanded significantly over the past five years—and that growth has strained the pool of qualified tradespeople. HVAC technicians, electricians, and roofing crews who are genuinely competent are running at capacity much of the year.

The implications for rental owners:

- **Availability:** Contractors you've never called before will push you to the back of the line. Account history matters. Contractors prioritize repeat customers and property managers they have ongoing relationships with.
- **Pricing:** A landlord without a vendor relationship pays full retail. A property manager or landlord with preferred vendor arrangements typically negotiates lower dispatch fees and time-and-materials rates.
- **Quality control:** Without a track record with a specific contractor, you're taking a risk on workmanship—a risk that's higher when you can't be on-site to supervise.

The solution: treat vendor relationships as a long-term asset. The CSRA landlords who get same-day HVAC service in July are the ones who called that HVAC company for a tune-up in April.

## What Georgia Law Requires Before a Contractor Touches Your Rental

Georgia has a dollar-threshold licensing rule that every rental owner needs to understand before hiring anyone.

Under [O.C.G.A. § 43-41-2](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-43/chapter-41/), a person must hold a Georgia Residential or General Contractor license when the total value of a project—labor and materials combined—exceeds **$2,500**. Below that threshold, unlicensed handyman work is generally legal, but only for tasks that don't affect the structural integrity or life-safety systems of the property.

What that means in practice for common rental repairs:

| Repair task | License required? |
|---|---|
| Water heater swap (under $2,500 total) | Generally no, but confirm local permit rules |
| HVAC repair under $2,500 | No, but EPA 608 cert required for refrigerant work |
| Any electrical panel or wiring work | Yes—licensed electrician required at any price |
| Roof replacement | Yes—over $2,500 in almost every case |
| Remodel or structural work over $2,500 | Yes—Georgia Residential Contractor license required |
| Drywall patch, door hardware, minor plumbing | No license required |

A separate provision, O.C.G.A. § 43-41-17(g), carves out "repair work" from the licensing requirement if the contractor discloses to the owner that they don't hold a Chapter 41 license and the work doesn't affect life safety or structural integrity. This is the practical basis for the "handyman exemption," but it doesn't cover electrical, gas, or structural work. When in doubt, require a license.

You can verify any Georgia contractor license through the [Georgia Secretary of State's licensing division](https://sos.ga.gov/page/residential-and-general-contractors-rules-and-regulations). Search by name or license number before writing the first check. The state also covers the difference between residential and general contractor classifications—make sure the license class matches the work.

## The Five Trade Categories Every CSRA Rental Owner Needs Covered

Build your vendor shortlist in these five categories. For each, you want at least two names—a primary and a backup—so you're never completely stuck when the first call doesn't go through.

### 1. HVAC

Non-negotiable in the CSRA. Augusta summers average high temperatures in the low 90s from June through September, and Georgia's Safe at Home Act places a habitability obligation on heating and cooling systems. When your HVAC fails, your tenant has a legal right to expect a timely remedy—and an HVAC claim that goes unresolved can expose you to constructive eviction liability.

Look for a company that holds an EPA 608 certification (required for refrigerant handling), offers a service call SLA for preferred customers, and can handle both emergency repair and planned maintenance. Annual tune-ups are your best insurance against emergency calls.

As of November 17, 2026, the three-bedroom homes we have listed in Evans are asking $1,895 to $2,400 a month. At that rent level, a single vacant month triggered by an unresolved HVAC dispute costs $2,100 on average—more than a preventive maintenance contract and a repair combined.

### 2. Plumbing

Plumbing issues are among the most common emergency calls in rental properties. Georgia's Safe at Home Act requires landlords to maintain hot water and sanitary plumbing in working order. Priority repair scenarios: water heater failures, supply line breaks, slow drains escalating to backups, and toilet seal failures that cause floor damage if left unaddressed.

A good residential plumber with CSRA rental experience will understand Georgia habitability timelines and won't leave a repair open for three days while your tenant has no hot water.

### 3. Electrical

License required, full stop. Electrical work in a rental property should go to a licensed master or journeyman electrician regardless of the dollar amount. Improperly done electrical work carries fire liability that your landlord insurance policy may not cover if unlicensed labor was involved. This is not the category to compromise on for price.

### 4. General Handyman / Light Repairs

For the many tasks under $2,500 that don't require a licensed trade—drywall repairs, door hardware, window re-glazing, appliance installation, exterior trim, caulking—a vetted residential handyman saves you money and turnaround time. The key word is vetted. "Vetted handyman" and "someone a Facebook friend recommended" are very different things.

Review the [Georgia Rental Habitability Checklist](/blog/georgia-rental-habitability-requirements-csra-landlords-2026/) for a full list of habitability items your handyman will need to handle competently. The Safe at Home Act requirements are non-waivable, which means your vendor's workmanship becomes your legal obligation.

### 5. Pest Control and Routine Exterior

Under Georgia's Safe at Home Act, landlords bear responsibility for pest infestations that affect habitability. A quarterly pest control contract—typically $80–$150 per visit for a standard single-family home in Columbia County—serves dual duty as both a habitability compliance tool and a property inspection touchpoint. Your pest control company will often be the first to notice an entry point, moisture intrusion, or structural gap that another vendor needs to address.

Lawn care is typically a tenant responsibility per most CSRA leases, but for vacant properties and during turnover, you need a reliable vendor you can call on short notice.

## A Seven-Point Vetting Process for Any CSRA Contractor

Before any vendor touches your property—new relationship or returning after a gap—run this checklist:

1. **Verify the Georgia license** (for trades requiring one). Search the Secretary of State's portal by company name or license number. Confirm the license class and that it's currently active.
2. **Request a certificate of insurance.** General liability (at least $500,000 per occurrence) and workers' compensation if they employ workers. The COI should name you as an additional insured and list the property address.
3. **Ask for three landlord references.** Specifically from other rental property owners or property managers, not homeowners. The kind of work done on a primary residence differs from rental-grade durable repairs.
4. **Check their BBB listing and Google Business profile.** Look at how they respond to negative reviews, not just the star count. A contractor who handles criticism professionally is more trustworthy than one who doesn't engage.
5. **Request a written estimate** before any work starts. The estimate should break out labor and materials separately—not just give you a lump sum.
6. **Ask about their coverage for incidental damage.** If they damage something beyond the repair scope, whose insurance covers it? A properly insured contractor will have a clear answer.
7. **Establish communication protocol.** How do they notify you when work is complete? Do they send photos? Can they accept payment via ACH? Set these expectations before the first job, not after.

None of this vetting is optional for licensed contractors, and all of it is worth applying to handyman work too. ## What to Put in Writing Before the First Job

A contractor who refuses to put the scope of work in writing before starting is a contractor you should walk away from.

At minimum, your written work agreement should include:

- **Scope:** Exactly what will be done, including the specific area of the property
- **Materials:** Brand, model number, or specification when material quality matters (e.g., paint grade, lumber grade, HVAC model number)
- **Price:** Total cost broken out by labor and materials
- **Timeline:** Projected start date and estimated completion date
- **Payment terms:** Typical is 50% upfront on jobs over $1,000, balance on completion after your inspection or photo review. Never pay 100% upfront to a contractor you haven't worked with before.
- **Warranty:** What is the warranty on labor? On parts they supply?

For recurring vendors—your quarterly HVAC tune-up company, your pest control service—a simple master service agreement covering standard rates, response times, and invoicing is worth drafting once and using permanently. ## Managing Your Vendor Network From Out of State

If you're a Fort Gordon PCS landlord stationed in a different time zone—or an out-of-state investor who bought in Evans without ever visiting Columbia County—vendor management is the hardest operational challenge you face. Distance strips you of the ability to show up, check the work, and have a direct conversation with the contractor.

A few practices that work:

**Photo-verified work orders.** Before authorizing a repair, require the contractor to send a photo of the problem. When the job is complete, require a photo of the finished work. This creates a documentary record and filters out contractors unwilling to engage with remote oversight.

**Spending authorization thresholds.** Set a dollar threshold—typically $200–$500—below which the contractor can proceed without your explicit approval, and above which they must call before starting. Document this in your contractor agreement and in your property's standing instructions. Our [Emergency Maintenance guide](/blog/emergency-maintenance-csra-rental-property-owner-guide/) covers how to structure this authorization with both contractors and tenants.

**Annual vendor list review.** Each fall, go through your vendor list and prune anyone you've had a problem with, confirm insurance and license renewals, and add backups where you have only one name in a category.

**Verification calls.** For jobs over $1,000, call the contractor before they start, not just after. Confirm the scope, the timeline, and the access arrangement. This takes five minutes and significantly reduces the chance of a miscommunication that becomes an expensive problem.
