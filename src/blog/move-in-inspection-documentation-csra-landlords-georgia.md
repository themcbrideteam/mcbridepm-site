## What Georgia Law Actually Requires

Georgia's inspection and security deposit statutes set the floor for landlord obligations — and define the consequences when those obligations aren't met.

[**O.C.G.A. § 44-7-33**](https://legis.ga.gov/api/legislation/document/20232024/202551) requires that before a landlord accepts a security deposit, they must provide the tenant with a written inspection form listing the existing condition of the dwelling. The tenant must be given a reasonable opportunity to sign it and receive a copy. If the landlord fails to provide this form, Georgia courts have consistently held that the landlord loses the right to make deductions — the entire deposit must be returned, regardless of actual damage.

**O.C.G.A. § 44-7-34** governs the return process. Within 30 days of the tenant vacating, the landlord must either return the full deposit or provide a written itemized list of deductions with supporting documentation. Each deduction must reference a specific condition in the property.

**O.C.G.A. § 44-7-35** gives the tenant 30 days after receiving your itemization to dispute your deductions.

**O.C.G.A. § 44-7-36** is the bad-faith penalty: if a court finds the landlord wrongfully withheld a deposit, the award can be up to three times the withheld amount plus reasonable attorney's fees. This isn't a theoretical risk — Columbia County and Richmond County magistrates apply it.

The statute says "written inspection form." It doesn't say "photo evidence" or "video walkthrough." But in practice, an unsigned form with no corroborating photos is nearly worthless the moment a tenant disputes it. The statute creates the legal framework; your documentation strategy determines whether you win or lose.

## Why Written Checklists Alone Aren't Enough

An uncorroborated written form creates a credibility problem the moment a tenant disputes it. Consider what happens in magistrate court:

You present a checklist saying "carpet — good condition" at move-in. The tenant presents photos they took showing what they claim is existing staining. Without your own timestamped, pre-possession photos of that specific carpet, the magistrate is weighing your word against theirs.

The written form documents your conclusions. Photos document the evidence underlying those conclusions. Courts expect both.

The same logic applies to walls, appliances, hardwood floors, bathroom fixtures, and exterior areas. Each item on your checklist needs visual backing that can be introduced as an exhibit. A checklist entry like "kitchen stove — good condition" means nothing if you can't show a photo of that stove taken before the tenant moved in.

The documentation standard that holds up in [Columbia County Magistrate Court](https://www.columbiacountyga.gov/government/courts/magistrate-court) and [Richmond County Magistrate Court](https://www.augustaga.gov/271/Courts) is: written form plus timestamped photos plus tenant signature plus copies distributed to both parties.

## The Photo and Video Protocol That Holds Up

The goal is to create an unambiguous pre-possession record that any third party — including a magistrate who's never seen the property — can understand and trust.

**Before photographing, confirm your timestamps.** Use a phone camera that embeds date, time, and GPS metadata in each image file (most modern smartphones do this automatically). Start each room by photographing the door or a card showing the room name and date — this creates a visual timestamp visible in any photo viewer, separate from the metadata.

**Minimum photo count for a 3-bedroom home:**

| Room | Minimum Photos |
|---|---|
| Each bedroom | 4 (two room angles + flooring close-up + closet interior) |
| Living room | 4 (two angles + flooring + any focal point) |
| Kitchen | 6 (overview + each appliance + inside cabinets + countertops) |
| Each bathroom | 4 (toilet, tub/shower, vanity, flooring) |
| Hallways | 2 |
| Laundry area | 2 (appliances if included, connections) |
| Garage/exterior | 4 (all four sides + driveway + HVAC unit) |
| Any noted defects | 2–3 close-up shots per defect |
| **Total (3BR/2BA)** | **60–80+ photos** |

**The video walkthrough.** Record a continuous single-pass video — narrate as you go. "Moving into the master bedroom — carpet is clean, no stains, no burns. Closet door operates smoothly. Window seal intact." The narration creates a spoken baseline that the photos support. Five minutes covering a 1,400 sq ft home is entirely typical and extremely defensible. Courts can play it back; they can't misread handwriting.

**Store everything in a cloud file tied to the lease.** Timestamps embedded in phone photos can be questioned if storage fails. Upload everything — photos, video, signed PDF — to a cloud folder or your property management system immediately after the inspection. For McBride Property Management clients, every move-in package uploads directly into the [AppFolio owner portal](/blog/appfolio-owner-portal-property-management-augusta-ga/) file for that lease, accessible to the owner at any time.

The main advantage over paper: consistent format across every unit and every inspection, with photos embedded directly in the report rather than stored separately and later mismatched.

**What digital signatures provide legally.** Georgia's Electronic Records and Signatures Act (O.C.G.A. § 10-12-1 et seq.) treats electronic signatures the same as wet signatures for most landlord-tenant purposes. A tenant who signs an inspection form via email link or mobile app has legally signed it.

**What digital signatures do NOT provide.** A digital signature on an emailed form doesn't prove the tenant physically walked through the property and agreed with the conditions noted. It's still far better than no signature — but the strongest documentation combines a digital or wet signature with a joint walkthrough where the tenant's observations are noted alongside yours.

**Comparing the main approaches:**

| Documentation Approach | Evidentiary Strength | Common Failure Point |
|---|---|---|
| Paper checklist, no photos, landlord only | Weak | No baseline; your word against tenant's |
| Paper checklist + photos, landlord only | Moderate | Tenant argues photos weren't pre-possession |
| Digital form + timestamped photos + tenant signature | Strong | None, if photos are timestamped and form correctly distributed |
| Digital form + photos + video + joint walkthrough + signature | Very strong | Requires coordination; best practice for high-value properties |

## Tenant Signature Requirements and What To Do When They Refuse

Under O.C.G.A. § 44-7-33, the tenant must have a "reasonable opportunity" to inspect and sign. Courts have interpreted this as: show them the form, explain what it covers, do a walkthrough, give them a chance to add observations before signing.

**At key handoff.** The ideal moment is at move-in key handoff — do the walkthrough together, tenant notes any additional defects, both parties sign. This is clean and legally clear.

**When the tenant insists on inspecting alone.** Some tenants prefer to walk through on their own and return the form within 24 hours. This is acceptable; include language in your lease acknowledging the 24-hour window and stating that failure to return the signed form within that period constitutes acceptance of the conditions as noted by the landlord. Many CSRA landlords include this clause in their standard [Georgia lease agreement](/blog/georgia-lease-agreement-clauses-csra-landlords-2026/) addendum.

**When the tenant flat-out refuses.** Document the refusal. Note the date and time, and if you have a witness, note them too. Write on the form itself: "Tenant declined to sign — [date] — [landlord signature]." Continue the tenancy but understand that your move-in documentation defense in any deposit dispute will rest on your photos and video alone. An unsigned form is weakened but not worthless if your photo and video evidence is strong.

**When the tenant disputes conditions on move-in.** Encourage this — it's legally healthy. A tenant who notes "bathroom tile has hairline crack" on the inspection form has actually helped you. They've acknowledged they saw and accepted that condition; it's now part of the record. If they note conditions you disagree with, sign the form with a written notation: "Landlord acknowledges tenant note; condition assessed as normal wear — [landlord initials]."

## Connecting Move-In Documentation to Move-Out Comparisons

The move-in inspection is only useful if it creates a referenceable baseline for the move-out comparison. Here's how to make that connection explicit.

**Use identical form structure.** Your move-in checklist and [move-out checklist](/pdfs/McBride-PM_Move-Out-Inspection-Checklist.pdf) should use the same room-by-room structure, same line items, and same condition ratings (Good / Fair / Poor / Damaged). When you produce the itemized deduction list under O.C.G.A. § 44-7-34, you can present them side by side: "Carpet — master bedroom: Move-in: Good condition. Move-out: Stained, pet odor penetration requiring replacement — cost $1,200."

**Pull move-in photos at move-out.** Before completing the move-out inspection, review every move-in photo. Walk the same route. Photograph the same angles. Where you see damage, capture it from an identical angle — the visual comparison is immediately legible to a magistrate.

**The 30-day clock.** From the date of surrender (tenants return all keys), you have 30 days under O.C.G.A. § 44-7-34 to return the deposit or mail the itemized deduction list. Missing this deadline — by even a day — typically forfeits your deduction rights regardless of the legitimacy of the damage. Build a calendar reminder into your process the moment keys are returned.

**Sending the itemization.** Use certified mail with return receipt for the written itemization. This creates a paper trail of delivery date, which matters if the tenant later claims they never received it and disputes the 30-day clock. This practice is covered in more depth in the [security deposit best practices guide for Georgia landlords](/blog/security-deposit-best-practices-georgia/) on this site.

## Checklist: 10 Move-In Documentation Steps CSRA Landlords Should Not Skip

This applies to self-managing landlords in Evans (ZIP 30809), Grovetown (ZIP 30813), Martinez (ZIP 30907), Augusta, Hephzibah, and throughout the CSRA. Work through it in sequence on move-in day.

- [ ] **Complete all repairs and cleaning before beginning the inspection** — document only the final condition the tenant will occupy
- [ ] **Record a continuous timestamped video walkthrough** — narrate room by room before the tenant arrives
- [ ] **Photograph every room from two angles** — minimum 60–80 photos for a standard 3-bedroom home
- [ ] **Photograph every appliance, cabinet interior, closet, light fixture, and window** — no room for "I forgot the kitchen cabinets"
- [ ] **Capture 2–3 close-up angles of any existing defect noted on the form** — wide shots alone won't prove specific damage
- [ ] **Complete the written inspection form with explicit condition ratings** — no blank lines; ambiguous blanks create disputes
- [ ] **Conduct a joint walkthrough with the tenant** — give them time to note any additional observations
- [ ] **Obtain tenant signature before or at key handoff** — both parties date and sign
- [ ] **Provide the tenant a copy immediately** — email with delivery confirmation, or in-person copy
- [ ] **Upload all documentation to a cloud file or PM system** — photos, video, and signed PDF together, labeled with the lease start date

[NARPM's professional standards](https://www.narpm.org) for property managers require this level of documentation as part of the owner's fiduciary duty. The same standard applies to self-managing landlords who want to protect their rights under Georgia law.

## What to Do If You're Already in a Deposit Dispute

If you're currently facing a dispute and your documentation is thin, there are still steps that help.

Gather everything you have. Dated text messages with the tenant referencing property conditions, work orders showing repairs completed before move-in, photos taken any time before possession — all of this is usable as corroborating evidence even if it doesn't meet best-practice standards.

Review the O.C.G.A. §§ 44-7-30 through 44-7-37 closely before responding to the tenant's dispute letter. Errors in the itemization letter — wrong amount, missed deadline, missing required language — can defeat an otherwise valid claim. The [security deposit guide for Georgia landlords](/blog/security-deposit-best-practices-georgia/) walks through the common pitfalls in the return process.

If the dispute escalates to magistrate court, consider consulting a Georgia landlord-tenant attorney before the hearing. This is general guidance from a property manager — not legal advice; a Georgia attorney can assess your specific documentation and advise on your prospects.

The easier path is building documentation standards now that make the dispute conversation short, because the photographic evidence is unambiguous.
