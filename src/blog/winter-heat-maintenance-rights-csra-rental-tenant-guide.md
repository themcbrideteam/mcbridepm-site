**Is your Georgia landlord required to maintain heat in your rental?**

## What Georgia Law Actually Says About Landlord Heat Obligations

**If your rental includes a heating system, your landlord must maintain it in working condition—Georgia law is clear on that, even if it doesn't set a minimum temperature.**

[O.C.G.A. § 44-7-13](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/2022/title-44/chapter-7/article-1/section-44-7-13/) is the foundation of Georgia landlord maintenance obligations. It requires landlords to keep rental property in repair and "in a fit and habitable condition." Georgia courts have consistently read this to cover essential mechanical systems—meaning if your unit has a furnace, heat pump, or baseboard heaters, those systems must be maintained in working order throughout your tenancy.

The [Georgia Safe at Home Act](https://gaappleseed.org/?p=4294) (HB 404), which took effect July 1, 2024 for new and renewed leases, strengthened this by creating an express warranty of habitability that no lease clause can waive. The Act specifically names "heat" as a utility that landlords must continue to furnish and maintain. No provision in your lease can remove this obligation. If a heating system exists in your CSRA rental, the law is unambiguous: it must work.

What Georgia law doesn't do is set a statewide minimum indoor temperature. Many states require landlords to maintain at least 68°F—Georgia has no equivalent number at the state level. Local housing codes in Augusta-Richmond County and Columbia County may impose additional requirements, but no county-specific temperature floor has been codified in widely accessible ordinances. The [Georgia DCA Landlord-Tenant Handbook](https://www.dca.ga.gov/safe-affordable-housing/rental-housing/landlord-tenant/resources) is the most reliable free reference for understanding the full scope of your rights.

Georgia law also doesn't require landlords who rented bare-bones units without any heating system to install one. This is a narrow situation—virtually all residential rentals in Evans, Grovetown, Martinez, Augusta, and North Augusta include HVAC—but it's worth knowing.

## Frozen Pipes: Who Pays and What You Should Do Right Now

**The landlord is responsible for the pipes and restoring service—your personal belongings are your responsibility, which is why renters insurance matters.**

Frozen and burst pipes are a real CSRA winter risk. Augusta's climate is mild, but temperatures below 25°F occur multiple times most winters, and older homes with pipes running through unheated crawl spaces, exterior walls, or unheated garages can freeze at 30°F when wind chill is a factor.

Here's how responsibility divides between landlord and tenant:

| Item | Who Is Responsible |
|---|---|
| Repairing the burst pipe | Landlord |
| Restoring water service to the unit | Landlord |
| Structural damage to walls or flooring from flooding | Landlord |
| Your damaged furniture, electronics, or clothing | You (renters insurance covers this) |
| Damage caused by your deliberate disabling of the heat system | You |

If a pipe bursts because the home had inadequate insulation or the landlord failed to maintain the heating system, those repair costs fall on the landlord under O.C.G.A. § 44-7-13 and the implied warranty of habitability. If you intentionally disabled the heating system and that caused the burst, your lease and Georgia law can shift liability to you.

**If a pipe bursts—act fast:**
1. Find and shut off the main water valve immediately. Know where it is *before* an emergency—if you're unsure, ask McBride PM.
2. Call **(706) 339-2874** to report the emergency.
3. Submit a written request in AppFolio with photos of the damage and of the water main (showing it's shut off).
4. Move valuables away from water if safe, but don't attempt DIY repairs on the building structure.
5. Document everything with time-stamped photos.

One note your landlord's insurance won't cover: your belongings. A single burst pipe can destroy thousands of dollars of furniture, clothing, or electronics. [Renters insurance in Georgia](https://mcbride-pm.com/blog/renters-insurance-georgia-what-it-covers/) typically runs $15–25 a month for most CSRA tenants and covers personal property loss from water damage. Many McBride PM leases require renters insurance—check yours.

## What to Do If Your Landlord Is Slow to Respond

**Georgia has no repair-and-deduct law, so never withhold rent. There are legitimate escalation paths that protect your position.**

If you've submitted a written maintenance request for a heating failure and haven't received a response in 24–48 hours during cold weather, here's the escalation sequence that keeps you legally protected:

**Step 1: Follow up in writing.** Send an email to robert@mcbride-pm.com restating the original request, the submission date, and the current conditions. Email is a dated record that holds up in magistrate court far better than a phone conversation.

**Step 2: Contact code enforcement.** If the property is in Richmond County (Augusta proper), call Augusta code enforcement at (706) 312-5050 to report an uninhabitable condition. Columbia County has its own code compliance division reachable through the county government website. Code enforcement can inspect and issue violations, which creates direct pressure on the property owner.

**Step 3: Seek a legal opinion.** The [Georgia DCA Landlord-Tenant Handbook](https://www.dca.ga.gov/safe-affordable-housing/rental-housing/landlord-tenant/resources) is a free, authoritative resource. A Georgia legal aid attorney can advise you on magistrate court options if the landlord has genuinely abandoned their habitability obligation.

**What not to do:** Do not withhold rent—even if the heat isn't working. Georgia has no repair-and-deduct statute. Withholding rent exposes you to a dispossessory filing for nonpayment, even when your underlying complaint is entirely legitimate. Address the heat problem through the channels above; address rent separately.

## If You Rent in Aiken or North Augusta: What South Carolina Law Adds

**South Carolina's landlord-tenant statute is more explicit about heat—and gives you a repair remedy Georgia tenants don't have.**

If your rental is in Aiken, North Augusta, Graniteville, Warrenville, or anywhere in South Carolina, you're under the [South Carolina Residential Landlord and Tenant Act (SCRLTA)](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/title27.php). SC Code § 27-40-440 explicitly requires landlords to maintain all heating facilities in good and working order—a clearer statutory standard than Georgia's more general "fit and habitable" language.

South Carolina also offers a remedy Georgia tenants don't have: the repair-and-deduct right under SC Code § 27-40-650. After providing proper written notice and allowing the landlord a reasonable period to act (typically 14 days for non-emergencies, shorter for genuine habitability threats), a tenant may arrange the repair and deduct the reasonable cost from rent—up to one month's rent.

| Standard | Georgia | South Carolina |
|---|---|---|
| Must maintain existing heat system? | Yes — O.C.G.A. § 44-7-13 | Yes — SC Code § 27-40-440 |
| Implied warranty of habitability? | Yes — Safe at Home Act, 2024 | Yes — SCRLTA § 27-40-230 |
| Statewide temperature minimum? | None | None |
| Repair-and-deduct right? | No | Yes — SC Code § 27-40-650, after proper written notice |
| Typical repair window, heat emergency | 24–48 hours (court practice) | Reasonable time; emergencies treated faster |

The repair-and-deduct path in SC is more procedurally complex than it looks—consult an SC-licensed attorney before pursuing it, and get written estimates before any repair is authorized. ## Winter Prep Checklist for CSRA Renters

**Test your heating system in October or early November—before you need it on a 35°F night at midnight.**

A little advance preparation saves everyone time and discomfort. Work through this list each fall before the first cold snap arrives:

**Before the season:**
- [ ] Turn on the HVAC in heat mode and run it for 20 minutes. Confirm warm air comes from every vent and that it reaches the set temperature.
- [ ] Check the air filter. A filter clogged with gray dust reduces efficiency and can trip the system's safety shutoff. Swap it out if needed—this is typically a tenant responsibility unless your lease says otherwise.
- [ ] Submit a non-emergency request for anything that sounds, smells, or behaves abnormally during your test run.
- [ ] Locate your water main shutoff valve. It's usually in the garage, a utility closet, or on an exterior wall near the water meter. Know where it is before pipes freeze.
- [ ] Test smoke detectors and carbon monoxide detectors. CO risk rises in winter—enclosed spaces and gas appliances operating under higher heating loads increase the danger.
- [ ] Check weatherstripping on exterior doors. If you feel a draft or see daylight around the frame, that's a maintenance request item—not your expense to fix.

**During a freeze warning (overnight forecast below 28°F):**
- [ ] Run a thin trickle of water from interior faucets on exterior walls. Moving water is much harder to freeze than standing water in a pipe.
- [ ] Open the cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls to allow warm air to circulate around the pipes.
- [ ] Keep the thermostat set to at least 55°F, even if you're away for the holiday. The energy cost is far less than a water damage claim.
- [ ] Close fireplace dampers when no fire is burning.

**If you're leaving for more than a few days:**
- [ ] Let McBride PM know you're away. The team can check on the property during cold snaps if they know the home is vacant.
- [ ] Never turn the heat completely off when below-freezing temperatures are in the forecast.
