**How much does a Georgia eviction really cost a CSRA landlord?**
Most owners focus on the $65–$75 court filing fee — but the real cost of a dispossessory in Augusta or Columbia County runs $2,000 to $8,000 when you count lost rent, make-ready, and re-leasing expenses. Contested cases regularly exceed $10,000.

You became a landlord without planning for it. A job relocation, a family situation, a home you couldn't sell at the price you needed — and now you're managing a rental property in Evans or Augusta while living somewhere else and doing something else. Most of the CSRA's accidental landlords fit some version of that description.

For a while, things ran fine. Then the rent stopped.

Before you start down the path of a dispossessory action, you need to understand what you're actually getting into — not just the court filing fee, but the full cost model. Because the $65 filing fee is a rounding error. The real number includes the months you weren't paid, the repairs you didn't expect, and the month of vacancy between tenants.

Here's the breakdown every CSRA accidental landlord needs before the first time things go sideways. If you haven't started the process yet, our [step-by-step Georgia eviction guide](/blog/georgia-eviction-process-landlord-guide/) covers the full procedure. This post covers what it costs.

## The 5-Category Model: Where the Money Actually Goes

There's no single number that defines an eviction's cost — there are five cost categories, and landlords who only watch the court fees are blindsided by the other four.

| Cost Category | Typical CSRA Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Court and legal fees | $90–$200 (DIY) / $600–$6,000 (with attorney) | Filing, service, writ execution |
| Lost rent during the process | $1,400–$3,400 | 1–2.5 months at CSRA 3BR average |
| Make-ready costs after vacancy | $800–$4,000+ | Depends heavily on tenant's condition of property |
| Vacancy carrying costs | $400–$1,200 | Mortgage, insurance, utilities while empty |
| Re-leasing costs | $1,000–$1,600 | Leasing fee plus time to fill |
| **Total: uncontested, average damage** | **$3,400–$7,000** | Most first evictions land here |
| **Total: contested or significant damage** | **$8,000–$15,000+** | |

Run these numbers against your property before you file. The question isn't just "can I win?" — it's "can I afford to win, and is a negotiated exit cheaper?"

## Category 1 — Court and Legal Fees

Court costs start small and compound fast once you add representation.

**Filing the dispossessory affidavit:** In Richmond County (Augusta) and Columbia County (Evans, Grovetown, Harlem), the filing fee runs approximately $65–$75. That gets your case on the docket. Per the [Columbia County Magistrate Court](https://columbiacountyga.gov/departments/courts/magistrate), you file the dispossessory affidavit at the clerk's office; the Augusta-Richmond County procedure is handled at the Magistrate Court at 735 James Brown Blvd.

**Sheriff service of process:** The tenant must be personally served. Expect $25–$50 for sheriff service in either county. If service fails — because a tenant is avoiding it — alias service costs more and delays your timeline by days or weeks.

**Writ of possession execution:** After you win, the sheriff executes the writ. An additional $50–$75 is typical. Some landlords budget for a constable or law enforcement presence during possession to prevent confrontation.

**Attorney representation:** This is where costs split dramatically.

If you represent yourself (pro se), total court costs might run $130–$200 for an uncontested dispossessory. Georgia magistrate court is designed to allow self-representation, and many CSRA landlords handle straightforward nonpayment cases without counsel.

Hire an attorney, and expect $500–$1,500 for an uncontested matter, $2,000–$5,000 for a contested case, and potentially more if the tenant files counterclaims or the case is transferred to state court on appeal. According to [iPropertyManagement's Georgia eviction cost research](https://ipropertymanagement.com/research/georgia-eviction-cost), attorney fees are the single largest variable in the total cost model and can turn a $200 case into a $3,000 case.

**One thing that surprises new landlords:** Georgia courts generally do not award attorney fees to the prevailing landlord unless the lease specifically provides for them and the court exercises its discretion to grant them. Don't count on recovering your legal costs.

*This is general guidance from a property manager — not legal or tax advice; talk to a Georgia attorney for your specific situation.*

## Category 2 — Lost Rent: Almost Always the Biggest Number

Here's the line item that shocks accidental landlords the first time.

The Georgia dispossessory process for nonpayment of rent — governed by [O.C.G.A. §§ 44-7-50 through 44-7-59](https://advance.lexis.com/documentpage/?pdmfid=1000516&crid=&pddocfullpath=%2Fshared%2Fdocument%2Fstatutes-legislation%2Furn%3AcontentItem%3A5YCB-3HH1-JS0R-20SY-00000-00&pdcontentcomponentid=234196&pdteaserkey=&pditab=allpods&ecomp=L5w_kkk&earg=sr4&prid=d4038c1e-1f69-4f8e-ac3b-a23e02aab00d) — begins with a written notice, then filing, then a court date. For leases entered or renewed on or after July 1, 2024, the [Safe at Home Act (HB 404)](https://www.legis.ga.gov/api/legislation/document/20232024/216456) added a mandatory 3-business-day pay-or-vacate notice before you can file. Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays don't count toward those three days.

After serving that notice and filing, here's a realistic timeline for Richmond County or Columbia County:

| Phase | Typical Duration |
|---|---|
| 3-day notice to pay or vacate | 3 business days (5–7 calendar days) |
| File dispossessory, court schedules service | 1–3 days |
| Sheriff service of process | 3–10 days |
| Tenant answer period | 7 days after service |
| Hearing date set and held | 3–10 days after answer filed |
| Writ of possession issued (if you win) | Same day or within 1–2 days |
| Writ execution by sheriff | Up to 7 days |
| **Total: uncontested nonpayment** | **4–7 weeks** |

For a CSRA 3-bedroom rental averaging $1,400–$1,700 per month, six weeks is $2,100–$2,550 in lost rent. That's the optimistic scenario.

Add a tenant who contests, requests a continuance, or appeals, and you're looking at 3–4 months. A 3-month eviction in Grovetown at $1,500/month is $4,500 in lost rent before any other expense.

One additional wrinkle: under O.C.G.A. § 44-7-50(c), a tenant served with a dispossessory for nonpayment can **tender all amounts owed within 7 days** and force dismissal of the case. If a tenant plays that card and falls behind again the following month, you restart the process. Budget for the possibility that a single troubled tenancy generates multiple rounds of procedure.

## Category 3 — Make-Ready Costs After the Writ

Tenants who stop paying rent are often the same tenants who leave the property in the worst condition. That's not universal — some nonpayment situations stem from temporary hardship and the tenant has cared for the home — but plan conservatively until you see the unit.

Common make-ready expenses after an eviction in the CSRA:

- **Professional cleaning:** $200–$500 for a typical 3-bedroom
- **Carpet cleaning:** $150–$300; full replacement at $2–$4/sq ft installed if condition warrants
- **Full repaint:** $800–$1,500 for a 1,400 sq ft home; touch-up is $200–$400 if only spot damage
- **Lock rekey or replacement:** $75–$150
- **Minor repairs** (doors, fixtures, hardware, cabinet hinges): $200–$600
- **Appliance repair or replacement:** $0–$1,500 depending on age and damage
- **Trash removal if personal property is abandoned:** $150–$400
- **Mold remediation if ventilation was neglected:** $500–$3,000+

A reasonable baseline for a standard post-eviction make-ready in Evans or Augusta: **$1,200–$2,500**. Properties with deliberate damage or prolonged deferred care routinely run $4,000–$8,000. The [McBride PM Operating Expenses Worksheet](/pdfs/McBride-PM_Operating-Expenses-Worksheet.pdf) includes a make-ready cost estimator that CSRA owners find useful when building their pre-eviction financial model.

You can attempt to recover make-ready costs from the security deposit and, separately, a money judgment. The deposit gives you documented, immediate recourse — but it often doesn't cover the full bill when several months of rent are already owed. Judges also require thorough documentation: written move-in inspection, dated photographs, itemized estimates. The [CSRA Landlord Field Guide](/pdfs/McBride-PM_CSRA-Landlord-Field-Guide.pdf) covers the documentation process that gives you the best shot at full recovery.

## Category 4 — Vacancy and Re-Leasing Costs

After the writ executes and the make-ready is complete, your unit is vacant. In the CSRA, a well-marketed, properly priced rental typically leases in 21–35 days. Add the make-ready period itself — minimum one to two weeks — and you're looking at an additional 4–6 weeks before rent resumes.

Carrying costs during vacancy on a typical CSRA single-family:

- **Mortgage on a $200,000 property at 7%:** approximately $1,330/month
- **Landlord insurance:** $130–$200/month prorated
- **Utilities** (minimum to maintain HVAC and security): $100–$200/month
- **Re-leasing fee if using a property manager:** typically 75–100% of one month's rent in the CSRA

At minimum, budget $1,500–$2,500 in carrying and re-leasing costs during the turnover gap.

## The Cash-for-Keys Alternative: Sometimes the Cheaper Path

Before you file, run the numbers on [cash for keys](/blog/cash-for-keys-vs-eviction-georgia-csra-landlords/). In some situations — particularly when a tenant owes 1–2 months of rent and has maintained the property reasonably well — a negotiated exit at $500–$1,500 paid to the tenant is the cheaper path. It's faster, it preserves the property, and it avoids the court record.

**When cash for keys pencils out:**
- Tenant is 1–2 months behind (smaller judgment to seek)
- Property appears well-maintained (low make-ready risk)
- Tenant is communicative and willing to cooperate
- Timeline savings of 4–6 weeks justify the payment

**When filing is the right call:**
- Tenant is 3+ months behind (substantial rent loss; judgment is worth pursuing)
- Property condition is unknown or suspected to be poor
- Tenant is non-responsive or has a documented history of dispute
- Money judgment is realistic to pursue or assign to collections

Our [cash for keys vs. eviction decision guide](/blog/cash-for-keys-vs-eviction-georgia-csra-landlords/) covers both paths with the specific CSRA magistrate court costs and a worked decision framework.

## Three Practices That Reduce Eviction Risk Before It Starts

The cheapest eviction is the one that never happens. Three practices have the highest ROI for CSRA accidental landlords who want to keep their worst-case scenario small.

### 1. Screen rigorously from the first applicant

[Tenant screening criteria](/blog/tenant-screening-criteria-income-credit-csra-landlords/) — verified income, credit check, rental history, and background — are the first line of defense. Georgia's Fair Housing rules set the outer boundaries, but within those boundaries you can require documented income at 3× monthly rent, a minimum credit score, and a verifiable rental history with no prior evictions.

The [McBride PM Tenant Screening Standards](/pdfs/McBride-PM_Tenant-Screening-Standards.pdf) are publicly available. Consistent, written, uniformly applied criteria are how you stay Fair Housing compliant while building a qualified tenant pool. The accidental landlords who skip this step to "just get someone in there" are the ones who call us six months later with a nonpayment situation.

### 2. Act at day one, not day thirty

There is no statutory grace period for rent in Georgia. If your lease says rent is due on the first, it is late on the second. The Safe at Home Act now requires 3 business days' written notice before filing — but that notice can go out on day two. Landlords who wait 30 or 45 days before sending any formal notice give up negotiating leverage and allow the debt to compound.

If you've recently become a landlord for the first time, the [Converting Your Georgia Home to a Rental checklist](/blog/converting-primary-residence-to-rental-georgia-checklist/) covers the operational steps — including setting late-rent protocols — that will keep you out of magistrate court.

### 3. Keep a funded make-ready reserve

A workable rule of thumb for CSRA single-family rentals: hold 1% of the property's value in a dedicated maintenance and turnover reserve. On a $200,000 Evans home, that's $2,000 — enough to cover an emergency make-ready without touching your mortgage or personal cash flow. Amber McBride, our operations manager, builds this conversation into every new owner onboarding so the reserve is funded before the first tenant moves in.
